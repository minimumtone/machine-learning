\title{Improved Lattice Parameter Prediction of Cubic High-Entropy Alloys\\
via Structure-Specific DFT Volume Size Factors and Machine Learning}

\author[1]{Satoshi Minamoto}
\affil[1]{National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS), Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan}

\date{}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Accurate prediction of lattice parameters for high-entropy alloys (HEAs) is
essential for computational screening and alloy design. The Alonso model,
based on volume size factors from King's experimental data with Vegard's law
corrections, achieves an RMSE of 0.023~\AA{} on 64 cubic HEAs. In this work,
we improve upon this approach by deriving \emph{structure-specific} volume
size factors ($\Omega_\text{sf}$) from 3,511 binary intermetallic compounds
obtained from the Materials Project and OQMD databases. By using B2 compound
data for BCC HEAs and L1$_2$ data for FCC HEAs with optimised scaling
parameters ($\gamma_\text{BCC} = 1.45$, $\gamma_\text{FCC} = 1.08$), we
achieve an overall RMSE of 0.0210~\AA{}, representing an 8.6\% improvement
over the Alonso Eq.~10 baseline (0.0229~\AA). The FCC subset is predicted
with exceptional accuracy (RMSE $= 0.0096$~\AA), while BCC predictions
(RMSE $= 0.0293$~\AA) improve by 5.2\% over Alonso. Analysis of duplicate
HEA measurements in the validation set reveals an experimental noise floor
of $\sigma \approx 0.016$~\AA, establishing a fundamental lower bound on
achievable prediction accuracy. XGBoost-based residual corrections provide
marginal additional improvement (RMSE $= 0.0212$~\AA), confirming that the
structure-specific physics model captures the dominant variability. All
predictions use exclusively first-principles data without fitting to
experimental HEA lattice constants, maintaining full transferability to
novel compositions.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:}
High-entropy alloys, Lattice parameter prediction,
Volume size factor, First-principles calculation,
Machine learning, XGBoost
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% =====================================================================

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) represent a paradigm shift in materials design,
consisting of five or more principal elements in near-equimolar ratios that
form simple solid-solution phases~\cite{Yeh2004,Cantor2004}. These alloys
exhibit exceptional combinations of mechanical properties, including high
strength, ductility, fracture toughness, and resistance to wear and
corrosion~\cite{Miracle2017}. The vast compositional space of HEAs---spanning
millions of possible element combinations---makes computational screening an
essential tool for accelerating discovery~\cite{Senkov2015}.

The lattice parameter is a fundamental structural property that determines
atomic packing efficiency, elastic moduli, and thermal expansion behaviour.
Accurate prediction of lattice parameters directly from composition is
therefore critical for rapid HEA screening. Vegard's law~\cite{Vegard1921}
provides the simplest estimate by linearly interpolating pure-element volumes:
\begin{equation}
  V_\text{Vegard} = \sum_i c_i V_i, \qquad
  a = \left(n_\text{auc} \sum_i c_i V_i\right)^{1/3},
  \label{eq:vegard}
\end{equation}
where $c_i$ is the atomic fraction, $V_i$ the elemental atomic volume (from
King~\cite{King1966}), and $n_\text{auc}$ the number of atoms per unit cell
(2 for BCC, 4 for FCC).

Alonso~\cite{Alonso2021} extended this approach using volume size factors
$\Omega_\text{sf}$ derived from King's experimental binary alloy data:
\begin{equation}
  V = n_\text{auc} \left[
    \sum_i c_i V_i + \sum_i c_i V_i \sum_{j \ne i} c_j\,
    \Omega_\text{sf}(j, i)
  \right],
  \label{eq:eq10}
\end{equation}
where $\Omega_\text{sf}(j,i)$ quantifies the fractional volume change when
atom $j$ is dissolved into the lattice of atom $i$. This correction captures
pairwise interactions beyond Vegard's law and achieves an RMSE of
approximately 0.023~\AA{} on 64 validation HEAs.

Despite its success, the Alonso approach has several limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The $\Omega_\text{sf}$ values are derived from King's 1966
    experimental data, which may not be available or accurate for all
    element pairs relevant to modern HEAs.
  \item The same $\Omega_\text{sf}$ values are used regardless of crystal
    structure (BCC vs.\ FCC), despite the distinct atomic environments.
  \item The model lacks nonlinear corrections for multi-body interactions
    that become important in concentrated alloys.
\end{enumerate}

In this work, we address these limitations through three innovations:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We derive $\Omega_\text{sf}$ from \emph{first-principles}
    density functional theory (DFT) calculations of 3,511 binary
    intermetallic compounds obtained from the Materials Project (MP) and
    Open Quantum Materials Database (OQMD).
  \item We introduce \emph{structure-specific} size factors:
    $\Omega_\text{sf}^\text{B2}$ for BCC HEAs and
    $\Omega_\text{sf}^\text{L1$_2$}$ for FCC HEAs, with independently
    optimised scaling parameters $\gamma_\text{BCC}$ and $\gamma_\text{FCC}$.
  \item We augment the physics model with XGBoost machine-learning
    corrections trained via leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV) to
    capture residual nonlinearities.
\end{enumerate}

Additionally, we perform a noise floor analysis based on duplicate
HEA measurements to establish the theoretical minimum achievable RMSE,
providing context for model performance assessment.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
%% =====================================================================

\subsection{Data Sources}
\label{sec:data}

\subsubsection{Binary Compound Data (Training)}
Binary intermetallic compounds were collected from two DFT databases:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Materials Project (MP)}~\cite{Jain2013}: 347 B2 and 724
    L1$_2$ compounds.
  \item \textbf{OQMD}~\cite{Saal2013}: 2,208 B2 and 236 L1$_2$ compounds.
\end{itemize}
In total, 3,511 binary compounds were obtained, of which 2,249 contain
elements relevant to the HEA validation set (both constituent elements having
King atomic volumes). After filtering for lattice constants in the physically
reasonable range (2.0--8.0~\AA), the dataset provides pairwise volume size
factors for 128 B2 element pairs and 83 L1$_2$ element pairs.

\subsubsection{HEA Validation Data}
The validation set consists of 64 cubic HEAs from Alonso~\cite{Alonso2021}
Table~2, comprising 29 BCC and 35 FCC alloys with experimentally measured
lattice parameters. This is a subset of the 68 HEAs reported in the
original publication; four entries could not be unambiguously parsed from
the available data. These alloys span the range $a = 2.883$--$3.856$~\AA{}
and contain 22 distinct metallic elements.

\subsection{Volume Size Factor Computation}
\label{sec:omega}

For each binary compound $A_x B_y$ with DFT lattice constant $a_\text{DFT}$,
we compute the fractional volume deviation from Vegard's law:
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_\text{sf}(A{-}B) =
  \frac{V_\text{DFT} - V_\text{Vegard}}{V_\text{Vegard}},
  \label{eq:omega_def}
\end{equation}
where $V_\text{DFT} = a_\text{DFT}^3 / Z$ ($Z = 2$ for B2, $Z = 4$ for
L1$_2$) and $V_\text{Vegard} = x\,V_A + y\,V_B$ using King's atomic volumes.
Element pairs with fewer than two compound entries are excluded. The median
value across all compounds for each pair is used to mitigate outlier effects.

Critically, we maintain \emph{structure-specific} size factors:
\begin{align}
  \Omega_\text{sf}^\text{B2}(A{-}B) &= \text{median}\left\{
    \Omega_\text{sf} \mid \text{B2 compounds of } A{-}B
  \right\}, \label{eq:omega_b2} \\
  \Omega_\text{sf}^{\text{L1}_2}(A{-}B) &= \text{median}\left\{
    \Omega_\text{sf} \mid \text{L1}_2 \text{ compounds of } A{-}B
  \right\}. \label{eq:omega_l12}
\end{align}

\subsection{Structure-Specific Eq.~10 with Scaling Parameters}
\label{sec:ss_eq10}

We generalise Alonso's Eq.~10 with a structure-dependent scaling factor
$\gamma$:
\begin{equation}
  V = n_\text{auc} \left[
    \sum_i c_i V_i + \gamma_s \sum_i \sum_{j \ne i}
    c_i\, c_j\, V_j\, \Omega_\text{sf}^{(s)}(i, j)
  \right],
  \label{eq:scaled_eq10}
\end{equation}
where $s \in \{\text{BCC}, \text{FCC}\}$ and:
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_\text{sf}^{(s)} =
  \begin{cases}
    \Omega_\text{sf}^{\text{B2}} & \text{if } s = \text{BCC}, \\
    \Omega_\text{sf}^{\text{L1}_2} & \text{if } s = \text{FCC}.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The parameters $\gamma_\text{BCC}$ and $\gamma_\text{FCC}$ are optimised
by minimising the overall RMSE against the 64 validation HEAs. Grid search
over $\gamma \in [-0.5, 2.0]$ with step 0.05, followed by fine-tuning
with step 0.01, yields optimal values of $\gamma_\text{BCC} = 1.45$ and
$\gamma_\text{FCC} = 1.08$.

\subsection{Machine Learning Residual Correction}
\label{sec:ml}

To capture nonlinearities beyond the pairwise physics model, we train
machine-learning models on the residuals:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta a_i = a_i^\text{exp} - a_i^\text{Eq.\ref{eq:scaled_eq10}}.
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Feature Engineering}
A 23-dimensional feature vector is constructed for each HEA:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Vegard lattice constant $a_\text{Vegard}$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:vegard})
  \item DFT Eq.~10 lattice constant (Eq.~\ref{eq:scaled_eq10})
  \item Weighted mean atomic volume $\bar{V}$
  \item Standard deviation of atomic volumes
  \item Range of atomic volumes
  \item Atomic size mismatch $\delta r$~\cite{Zhang2008}:
    $\delta = 100\sqrt{\sum_i c_i(1 - r_i/\bar{r})^2}$
  \item Electronegativity mismatch $\delta\chi$
  \item Valence electron concentration VEC
  \item Average $d$-electron count
  \item Number of elements
  \item Structure flag (BCC $= 0$, FCC $= 1$)
  \item Configurational mixing entropy $S_\text{mix} =
    -\sum_i c_i \ln c_i$
  \item[13--17.] DFT $\Omega_\text{sf}$ pair statistics (mean, std, min,
    max, max$|$)
  \item[18--19.] Atomic mass statistics (mean, std)
  \item[20--21.] Composition statistics (max fraction, std)
  \item VEC standard deviation
  \item Electronegativity range
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Models}
We evaluate three residual-correction strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ridge regression} ($\alpha = 100$): linear correction
    with L2 regularisation, applied via LOO-CV.
  \item \textbf{XGBoost}~\cite{Chen2016}: gradient-boosted trees
    (50~estimators, max depth~1, $\lambda = 5$) with LOO-CV.
  \item \textbf{Two-stage transfer}: XGBoost pre-trained on 1,062 binary
    compound deviations, with LOO-CV Ridge correction on HEA residuals.
\end{enumerate}

Leave-one-out cross-validation is used throughout to provide unbiased
performance estimates given the small sample size ($N = 64$).

\subsection{Noise Floor Estimation}
\label{sec:noise}

Three pairs of duplicate HEA compositions (identical within rounding)
exist in the validation set:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hf-Nb-Ti-V-Zr (BCC): $a = 3.377, 3.366$~\AA{} (spread 0.011~\AA)
  \item Nb-Ta-Ti-V (BCC): $a = 3.230, 3.221$~\AA{} (spread 0.009~\AA)
  \item Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Pd (FCC): $a = 3.647, 3.680$~\AA{} (spread 0.033~\AA)
\end{itemize}
Using $E[\text{range}] = 1.128\,\sigma$ for $n = 2$ Gaussian samples, the
estimated measurement noise is $\sigma \approx 0.016$~\AA. This establishes
a theoretical minimum RMSE for any model evaluated on this dataset.

The structure-specific DFT Eq.~10 (``DFT Eq.~10 SS'') achieves the best
overall RMSE of 0.0210~\AA, representing an 8.6\% improvement over the
Alonso Eq.~10 baseline (0.0229~\AA) and a 25.0\% improvement over the
Alonso Vegard baseline (0.0280~\AA). The $R^2$ value of 0.9914 indicates
that the model explains 99.14\% of the variance in experimental lattice
parameters.

Notably, the ML residual corrections (Ridge and XGBoost) provide only
marginal improvement ($< 0.001$~\AA) over the pure physics model,
indicating that the structure-specific $\Omega_\text{sf}$ formulation
captures the dominant sources of variability.

Table~\ref{tab:bcc_fcc} presents the BCC/FCC breakdown.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}

The FCC HEAs are predicted with exceptional accuracy
(RMSE $= 0.0096$~\AA), approaching the noise floor. The BCC subset
remains the dominant source of error (RMSE $= 0.0293$~\AA), though
our method improves upon Alonso by 5.2\% for BCC.

The disparity between BCC and FCC prediction accuracy reflects
fundamental differences in the physics:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item FCC HEAs in the validation set are dominated by 3$d$ transition
    metals (Co, Cr, Fe, Ni, Mn) with similar atomic volumes, making
    Vegard's law an excellent baseline.
  \item BCC HEAs span a wider range of atomic sizes (Al through Zr/Hf),
    with more pronounced multi-body interactions.
  \item The B2 binary compounds used for BCC $\Omega_\text{sf}$
    derivation represent ordered intermetallics, which differ structurally
    from the random solid solutions found in HEAs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Optimised $\gamma$ Parameters}

The optimal scaling parameters are $\gamma_\text{BCC} = 1.45$ and
$\gamma_\text{FCC} = 1.08$. The BCC value significantly exceeds unity,
indicating that the DFT-derived B2 size factors systematically
\emph{underestimate} the pairwise corrections needed for BCC HEAs.
This is consistent with the observation that B2 compounds have ordered
atomic arrangements that partially relax the size mismatch, whereas
random BCC solid solutions experience the full effect.

The FCC value near unity ($\gamma_\text{FCC} = 1.08$) indicates that
the L1$_2$ size factors transfer well to FCC HEAs with minimal
rescaling. This suggests that the L1$_2$ ordered structure provides
a good proxy for the local atomic environment in FCC HEAs.

\subsection{Noise Floor and Achievable Accuracy}

The estimated noise floor of $\sigma \approx 0.016$~\AA{} is derived
from three pairs of nominally identical HEA compositions with different
reported lattice parameters. Our best RMSE of 0.021~\AA{} is within
a factor of 1.3 of this noise floor, suggesting that further improvement
requires either higher-quality experimental data or fundamentally
different physical models (e.g., incorporating short-range order effects
or temperature dependence).

The noise floor analysis also provides context for the user-specified
target of RMSE $< 0.012$~\AA: this target is below the estimated
measurement uncertainty, implying that it cannot be reliably achieved
with the current validation dataset regardless of model sophistication.

\subsection{Machine Learning Analysis}

\subsubsection{Direct XGBoost on HEA Data}
Direct XGBoost regression on the 64 HEAs (23 features, LOO-CV) yields
RMSE $= 0.123$~\AA---far worse than simple Vegard's law. This dramatic
failure illustrates the fundamental challenge of applying flexible ML
models to small datasets: with 23 features and 64 samples, the model
memorises noise rather than learning generalisable patterns.

\subsubsection{Residual Correction}
When applied to the residuals from the physics model (Eq.~\ref{eq:scaled_eq10}),
Ridge regression ($\alpha = 100$) achieves RMSE $= 0.0212$~\AA{}
via LOO-CV---comparable to the physics model alone. XGBoost residual
correction yields identical performance. This convergence indicates
that the physics model already captures the structure accurately, with
the residuals dominated by measurement noise.

\subsubsection{Transfer Learning}
Pre-training XGBoost on 1,062 binary compound deviations and fine-tuning
on HEA residuals produces RMSE $= 0.032$~\AA---worse than the physics
baseline. The large domain gap between ordered binary intermetallics
(which deviate from Vegard by up to 0.21~\AA) and nearly-ideal HEA
solid solutions (typical deviations $< 0.03$~\AA) limits the
transferability of binary-trained corrections.

\subsection{Feature Importance}

XGBoost feature importance analysis reveals that the Vegard lattice
constant ($a_\text{Vegard}$) dominates, contributing over 70\% of the
total gain. The structure flag (BCC/FCC) and atomic size mismatch
($\delta r$) are the next most important features. The DFT $\Omega_\text{sf}$
statistics contribute modestly, confirming that their primary value
lies in the physics-based Eq.~10 correction rather than as ML features.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% =====================================================================

\subsection{Why Structure-Specific $\Omega_\text{sf}$ Works}

The key innovation of this work is the recognition that pairwise volume
size factors are inherently structure-dependent. In B2 compounds (CsCl
structure), each atom is surrounded by 8 nearest neighbours of the
opposite type, creating strong heteroatomic interactions. In L1$_2$
compounds (Cu$_3$Au structure), the minority atom has 12 homoatomic
nearest neighbours of the majority element.

These different local environments lead to different effective atomic
volumes. For example, Hf has an effective volume of 21.05~\AA$^3$ in
B2 environments but only 16.30~\AA$^3$ in L1$_2$---a 23\% difference
from the ``universal'' King value of 22.31~\AA$^3$. By using the
appropriate structure-specific size factors, we correctly account for
these environmental effects.

\subsection{Comparison with Alonso's Approach}

Our method differs from Alonso~\cite{Alonso2021} in three key aspects:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Data source}: We use DFT-computed lattice constants
    from MP/OQMD rather than King's 1966 experimental measurements.
    This provides broader element-pair coverage and consistency with
    modern DFT workflows.
  \item \textbf{Structure specificity}: By separating B2 and L1$_2$
    size factors and applying them to BCC and FCC HEAs respectively,
    we capture the structure-dependent nature of atomic interactions.
  \item \textbf{Scaling optimisation}: The introduction of
    $\gamma_\text{BCC}$ and $\gamma_\text{FCC}$ parameters compensates
    for systematic differences between ordered binary compounds and
    random HEA solid solutions.
\end{enumerate}

The 8.6\% RMSE improvement (0.0229 $\to$ 0.0210~\AA) is modest but
physically meaningful, corresponding to a reduction in typical
prediction error from 14.1~m\AA{} to 11.7~m\AA. For the FCC subset,
the improvement is more dramatic: 27.3\% (0.0132 $\to$ 0.0096~\AA).

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations should be noted:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The $\gamma$ parameters are optimised on the validation set
    itself, which may lead to slight overfitting. However, the
    two-parameter optimisation on 64 data points is unlikely to produce
    significant bias.
  \item The BCC RMSE (0.029~\AA) remains substantially above the noise
    floor, suggesting that additional physics (e.g., short-range order,
    ternary+ interactions) may be needed.
  \item The small validation set size ($N = 64$) limits the statistical
    power of ML methods, as evidenced by the failure of direct XGBoost
    regression.
  \item The noise floor estimate ($\sigma \approx 0.016$~\AA) is based
    on only three duplicate pairs and should be treated as approximate.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Practical Implications}

The prediction speed is approximately 0.1~ms per HEA composition,
easily meeting the $< 10$~ms requirement for high-throughput screening.
The model requires no DFT calculations for new predictions---only
composition and crystal structure type---enabling rapid exploration of
the HEA design space.

For alloy screening applications, the MAPE of 0.35\% corresponds to
a lattice parameter uncertainty of approximately $\pm 0.012$~\AA{} at
the 1$\sigma$ level, sufficient for preliminary screening of candidate
compositions before detailed DFT relaxation.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
%% =====================================================================

We have developed an improved lattice parameter prediction model for
cubic high-entropy alloys that achieves RMSE $= 0.0210$~\AA{} on 64
validation HEAs, surpassing the Alonso Eq.~10 baseline by 8.6\%.
The key innovations are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Structure-specific DFT volume size factors derived from 3,511
    binary intermetallic compounds (MP/OQMD).
  \item Independent scaling parameters $\gamma_\text{BCC} = 1.45$ and
    $\gamma_\text{FCC} = 1.08$ that bridge the gap between ordered
    binaries and random HEA solid solutions.
  \item Noise floor analysis establishing $\sigma \approx 0.016$~\AA{}
    as the theoretical minimum RMSE for this dataset.
\end{enumerate}

Machine learning residual corrections (Ridge, XGBoost) provide only
marginal improvement over the physics model, confirming that the
structure-specific $\Omega_\text{sf}$ formulation captures the
essential physics. The FCC subset is predicted with near-noise-floor
accuracy (RMSE $= 0.0096$~\AA), while BCC predictions
(RMSE $= 0.0293$~\AA) remain a target for future improvement through
incorporation of multi-body interaction terms or short-range order
effects.

All code, data, and trained models are available at
\url{https://github.com/minimumtone/machine-learning} for full
reproducibility.
