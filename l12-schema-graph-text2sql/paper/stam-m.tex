\begin{abstract}
Inorganic materials databases presuppose SQL knowledge, creating a significant barrier to data utilization for materials researchers.
To remove this barrier, we present the design principles for a Text-to-SQL pipeline that enables natural language access to inorganic materials relational databases (RDBs) and demonstrate its effectiveness.
The proposed method comprises seven components: (i) condition extraction with a materials domain dictionary, (ii) LLM-based schema linking (table prioritization), (iii) few-shot example retrieval, (iv) JOIN constraints via greedy shortest-path connection on the foreign-key (FK) graph, (v) $n$-best SQL generation by a large language model, (vi) AST-based SQL safety verification (SQLGuard), and (vii) an LLM reranker.

Using a 31-table RDB centered on L1$_2$ and covering five prototypes (B2/NaCl/NiAs/BiF$_3$; roughly 20,000 rows in total, 1{,}559 entries in the central table), a multi-axis evaluation achieved execution recall of 85.6\%, precision of 84.7\%, and F1 of 79.3\%.
SQLGuard rejects non-SELECT statements and references to unauthorized tables before execution, and syntax validity was 100\% in the reported evaluation; this value does not guarantee safety in general but reflects the static verification items and adversarial inputs defined in this study.
Four generalization tests yielded execution recall of 95.0\% for cross-prototype queries on the same schema (A), 95.0\% for an OQMD-flavored rename (B), 90.0\% for identifier obfuscation (C), and 100.0\% for a three-table schema built from real Materials Project data (D). A verifies domain diversity within the same DB, B and C are schema transfers with no code changes, and D is a lightweight adaptation using an MP-specific prompt and eight few-shot examples; the four are not uniform ``zero-adaptation'' tests.
The central finding of a 7-condition ablation study with 100 evaluation queries (5 independent runs) is that domain adaptation through few-shot examples governs accuracy ($-$5.4\,pp, $p=0.001$, significant after Holm correction over six comparisons). The materials domain dictionary ($-$4.9\,pp, $p=0.029$) and the reranker ($-$5.1\,pp, $p=0.016$) are significant uncorrected but do not reach the significance level after Holm correction. The FK-graph JOIN constraint did not change overall accuracy at this scale (31 tables, with the entire schema fitting in the prompt; $\pm$0.0\,pp, query-level bootstrap 95\%CI $[-0.6, +0.6]$\,pp), and its main role is maintainability through FK introspection.
On 15 CTE multi-step calculation patterns (including formation-enthalpy calculation from pure-element references), the five known patterns covered by few-shot examples reached 100\% while the ten novel patterns without examples reached 40.3\% (60.2\% overall), showing that multi-step calculation coverage is determined by few-shot pattern coverage. Together with an additional validation on 100 independently designed queries (execution recall 72.7\%), these results demonstrate the feasibility and applicability conditions of a research infrastructure in which materials researchers perform cross-table exploration and engineering-oriented queries without writing SQL.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\paragraph*{Barriers to data exploration in inorganic materials research}

Inorganic materials research is shifting toward data-driven materials discovery, propelled by advances in high-throughput first-principles calculations. Databases such as OQMD~\cite{saal2013oqmd,kirklin2015oqmd}, Materials Project~\cite{jain2013mp}, AFLOW~\cite{curtarolo2012aflow}, NOMAD~\cite{draxl2019nomad}, and AtomWork-Adv~\cite{nims2018atomworkadv} store tens to hundreds of thousands of computational records covering composition, crystal structure, electronic states, and thermodynamic stability.

These data are typically stored in normalized relational databases (RDBs) designed for flexible cross-table querying with SQL. Although most DFT databases also provide APIs, APIs are ill-suited to complex conditional searches spanning multiple tables and to large-scale statistical processing. SQL---and Text-to-SQL, which generates SQL from natural language---can directly exploit database relations and thus respond flexibly to exploratory extraction and aggregation. However, using an RDB requires understanding table structures, foreign-key (FK) relationships, and SQL syntax, which constitutes a major barrier for materials researchers who know the data exist but cannot access them. As a result, valuable data remain underutilized.

\paragraph*{Significance of natural language database interfaces}

The essence of this barrier is that the technical requirement of writing SQL stands between domain knowledge and the data. In normalized materials RDBs, constructing multi-table JOINs is difficult even for experienced SQL users; if appropriate SQL can be generated automatically from natural language, materials researchers can perform cross-table data exploration without being conscious of schema structure. Furthermore, a design that relies on FK metadata makes adaptation to schema evolution comparatively easy.

\paragraph*{Text-to-SQL technology and the current state in materials science}

Text-to-SQL technology has developed around benchmarks such as Spider~\cite{yu2018spider} and BIRD~\cite{li2024bird}. With recent advances in large language models (LLMs), DIN-SQL~\cite{pourreza2024dinsql} proposed a pipeline approach separating schema linking, classification, and generation. DAIL-SQL~\cite{gao2023dail} focused on few-shot example selection strategies. RAT-SQL~\cite{wang2020ratsql} introduced relation-aware encoding, and RESDSQL~\cite{li2023resdsql} introduced decoupled schema linking. SchemaGraphSQL~\cite{safdarian2026schemagraphsql} is closest to our work in that it uses path search on the FK graph.

However, these methods have been evaluated on general benchmarks, and their effectiveness on domain-specific RDBs such as inorganic materials databases remained unverified. MatSciBERT~\cite{gupta2022matscibert} and MatBERT~\cite{walker2021matbert} are language models for materials science text but have not been directly applied to Text-to-SQL. MKNA~\cite{zhuang2026mkna} and OptiMat~\cite{hu2026optimat} provide API-based access but do not perform SQL generation with JOIN control over normalized RDBs. SuperCon~\cite{ishii2023supercon} and PoLyInfo~\cite{ishii2024polyinfo} adopt RDF/SPARQL-based ontology approaches with strong semantic reasoning, but applying them to existing RDB infrastructure requires data conversion. Moreover, a configuration in which a bare LLM is given only schema information and made to generate SQL directly suffers frequent hallucination of nonexistent tables and columns and incomplete condition specification (concrete generated-SQL examples are shown in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_sql_examples}), and is not practically usable as is. Addressing this problem is the starting point of this study.

\paragraph*{Objectives and contributions}

The objective of this study is to present design principles that enable natural language access to inorganic materials RDBs and to verify their effectiveness. We present a methodology by which materials researchers can explore data without awareness of SQL or schema structure, and examine, using an RDB built from data extracted from OQMD as an example, to what extent natural language queries can be handled.

The three domain-specific challenges addressed in this study are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Ambiguity of domain terminology:
    Crystal-structure names and property terms such as L1$_2$, the $\gamma'$ phase, and B2 are underrepresented in general LLM training data, making accurate translation into SQL conditions difficult.
  \item Complexity of multi-table JOINs:
    Normalized materials RDBs require JOINs across dozens of tables, raising the risk of JOIN hallucination by LLMs.
  \item Lack of evaluation data:
    No standard evaluation benchmark exists for Text-to-SQL on inorganic materials RDBs. Valid evaluation requires difficulty calibration, and benchmark design requires dialogue with domain experts.
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are fourfold:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item Design principles for materials RDB Text-to-SQL:
    We propose a pipeline whose core is domain adaptation via a materials domain dictionary and few-shot examples, integrated with JOIN constraints via greedy shortest-path connection on the FK graph (suppressing JOIN hallucination and aiding maintainability) and a hybrid reranker.
    This bridges the gap between general Text-to-SQL methods lacking materials-specific term translation (DIN-SQL, DAIL-SQL, SchemaGraphSQL, etc.) and materials NL access systems not targeting RDB/SQL (MKNA, OptiMat, etc.).
  \item Multi-axis quantitative evaluation:
    Beyond a statistical ablation of 7 conditions $\times$ 100 queries $\times$ 5 runs (query-level sign-flip tests and bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals) on a 31-table RDB with roughly 20,000 rows covering five prototypes centered on L1$_2$, we provide few-shot $k$ sensitivity, dictionary-size sensitivity, LLM model comparison, and multi-axis metrics (EM / SELECT-column precision / JOIN match rate).
  \item Quantitative effects of domain knowledge:
    We show that few-shot examples ($-$5.4\,pp, $p=0.001$, significant after Holm correction) make the largest contribution, and that the materials dictionary ($-$4.9\,pp, $p=0.029$) and the reranker ($-$5.1\,pp, $p=0.016$) are also significant uncorrected, validating the necessity of domain-specific modules.
  \item Demonstration of schema generalization:
    We compare four generalization tests under differing conditions---A~cross-prototype queries on the same schema (95.0\%), B~an OQMD-flavored rename (95.0\%), C~randomized obfuscation (90.0\%), and D~a separate schema built from real Materials Project data (100.0\%). These indicate potential extensibility beyond the specific 31-table configuration, while A is not a schema transfer and D uses MP-specific few-shot examples, so the strength of generalization is interpreted separately per variant (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_eval}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Pipeline Overview}

To enable natural language access to inorganic materials RDBs, we designed the pipeline shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. The pipeline receives a natural language query (Japanese) as input, performs materials-domain-term analysis, automatic schema traversal, and SQL generation and verification, and returns execution results. Below, each component (Sections~\ref{sec:entity_extractor}--\ref{sec:repair}) is described following the processing order of Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. Among these, the condition extractor and the materials domain dictionary (Sections~\ref{sec:entity_extractor}--\ref{sec:domain_dict}) are the modules that address challenges specific to materials RDBs. Evaluation queries are classified into four difficulty tiers---Easy / Medium / Hard / Very Hard (VH)---based on the structural complexity of the gold SQL (definitions in Section~\ref{sec:query_design}).

\subsection{Few-shot Example Retrieval}
\label{sec:fewshot}

Few-shot examples similar to the input query are selected in two stages.
First, TF-IDF~\cite{salton1988tfidf} narrows the candidates to $2k$ items,
and then a cross-encoder (ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2; a small model that scores semantic similarity given a query--candidate pair) selects the top $k$.
The cross-encoder runs locally with processing time under 50\,ms.

The selected few-shot examples demonstrate SQL generation patterns such as table names, JOIN order, and column-name usage. Through in-context learning~\cite{brown2020fewshot}, the LLM thereby stabilizes generation grounded in concrete examples that are not determined by schema information alone.

\subsection{Schema Linking}
\label{sec:schema_link}

In this paper, ``schema linking'' refers, following standard usage in the Text-to-SQL field, only to the association of natural language elements with schema elements (in our implementation, LLM-based scoring and sorting of tables). The static condition-to-column mapping table of Section~\ref{sec:domain_dict} is called the ``schema mapping dictionary,'' and the two are distinguished. GPT-5.5 scores and sorts the tables relevant to the input query. Tables are not excluded; only the context priority order for SQL generation is changed.

\subsection{SQL Generation and $n$-best Candidates}
\label{sec:nbest}

GPT-5.5 outputs the top $n$ SQL candidates (default $n$-best = 3) in parallel in a single generation call, and the downstream reranker selects the best one. Increasing the number of candidates widens the interpretation space but also increases latency.
\subsection{Greedy Shortest-Path JOIN Constraint on the FK Graph}
\label{sec:schema_graph}

FK relationships among the 31 tables are represented as an undirected graph $G=(V,E)$ ($V$ is the set of tables, $E$ the set of FK relationships) using NetworkX~\cite{networkx2008}. The graph is undirected and may contain cycles; acyclicity is not required (the JOIN subgraph is constructed as a union of shortest paths connecting the required tables). FK relationships are obtained at runtime from PostgreSQL metadata. The implementation examines each pair of required tables in turn, adds the shortest path on the FK graph for pairs not yet in the same connected component, and updates connectivity with Union--Find (Algorithm~\ref{alg:join_connector}). This is not an exact or standard approximation algorithm for the greedy shortest-path connector but a greedy heuristic for connecting required tables via a small number of FK paths. It therefore does not guarantee that the resulting JOIN subgraph is always globally minimal.

Implementation pseudocode, the FK extraction SQL from \texttt{information\_schema}, EXISTS-subquery expansion of multi-element AND conditions, and JOIN-clause generation details are given in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_method_details}. At the 31-table scale (number of required tables $k \leq 6$), the connection completes in milliseconds, and because FK metadata is fetched at runtime, schema changes are automatically reflected in the graph.

\subsection{Condition Extractor}
\label{sec:entity_extractor}
\label{sec:coverage_score}
\label{sec:intent_classifier}

The condition extractor converts a natural language query into a structured condition dictionary using a bilingual (Japanese--English) term dictionary (\texttt{material\_terms.yaml}) that reflects the table and column structure of the target RDB. The dictionary covers four categories: (1)~prototype aliases (e.g., \texttt{L12} $\leftrightarrow$ [L1$_2$, Cu$_3$Au type, $\gamma'$]), (2)~element mappings, (3)~stability terms (``stable'' $\to$ \texttt{energy\_above\_hull} $\leq 0.001$~eV/atom), and (4)~property terms. The numeric-condition parser recognizes five patterns including symbolic comparison, Japanese postpositions, sign determination, and range specification, and chemical-formula tokens are classified contextually into exact\_formula / reduced\_formula / contains\_elements (EXISTS expansion). A coverage score ($[0,1]$), the fraction of recognized semantic tokens, prevents silent constraint dropping and routes execution to rule-based mode at $\geq 0.8$, LLM mode at $\geq 0.5$, and a user-confirmation request below $0.5$. In addition, a rule-based intent classifier pre-filters out-of-scope questions. See Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_method_details} for the pattern table and ambiguity handling.

\subsection{Materials Domain Dictionary}
\label{sec:domain_dict}

We introduced a dictionary that maps materials-science terms directly to SQL conditions, e.g., ``$\gamma'$ phase'' $\to$ \texttt{structure.prototype = 'L12'}, and ``bulk modulus'' $\to$ \texttt{property\_name = 'bulk\_modulus'} in the \texttt{calculated\_property} table. Note that the $\gamma'$ phase has different contexts in steels versus Ni-based superalloys; in this paper it is treated as a phase name equivalent to the L1$_2$ prototype.

The dictionary is compiled from three sources:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item YAML definition file (\texttt{material\_terms.yaml}): 74 terms
  \item Pipeline vocabulary (DB/OQMD-specific terms): 66 terms
  \item Materials engineering vocabulary (user-provided list): 402 terms
\end{enumerate}
After deduplication, the compiled vocabulary contains 492 terms. Of these, what is used directly in the execution path of the evaluation pipeline is (a)~the bilingual term dictionary for term-to-condition extraction (\texttt{material\_terms.yaml}, applied via regular-expression and substring matching), and (b)~the schema mapping dictionary from conditions to tables, columns, and SQL fragments (a 61-entry condition-to-column mapping table; distinct from the LLM-based schema linking of Section~\ref{sec:schema_link}. The full entry list is Table~\ref{tab:schema_map_dict} in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_method_details}). The size-sensitivity experiment of Table~\ref{tab:dict_size} was measured by simultaneously and progressively reducing this 61-entry schema mapping dictionary and the term dictionary, so the observed degradation is not interpreted as the causal effect of the 61-entry dictionary alone.

The 492-term vocabulary was also compiled as a custom dictionary for the MeCab morphological analyzer~\cite{kudo2004mecab}, improving the single-token rate of 402 materials terms from 30.6\% to 100.0\% (Table~\ref{tab:mecab}; Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_method_details}). This is a resource for measuring tokenization quality; morphological analysis is not used in the execution path of the evaluation pipeline (term matching is by direct dictionary lookup).

The dictionary improves interpretation of Hard and above queries, especially the VH class containing domain jargon and multi-step conditions. Combining the dictionary with few-shot examples is beneficial for improving Hard/VH-tier accuracy.
\subsection{SQLGuard}
\label{sec:sqlguard}

SQLGuard parses generated SQL into an abstract syntax tree (AST) using sqlglot~\cite{sqlglot2023}, a Python SQL toolkit for parsing, AST analysis, and dialect conversion, and statically verifies safety and consistency by traversing the AST nodes (\texttt{Statement}, \texttt{Select}, \texttt{Column}, \texttt{Table}, \texttt{Join}, etc.). AST analysis via sqlglot enables structural confirmation of multi-statement injection, table/column reference resolution, and subquery nesting depth, which are hard to determine at the text level. SQL failing any check is rejected before execution. The verification consists of 15 checks: multi-statement detection, forbidden keyword (DML/DDL) detection, SELECT-only confirmation, dangerous function prohibition, system table reference prohibition, SELECT-only confirmation of CTE bodies, tautology (injection pattern) detection, automatic LIMIT addition, table and column existence checks, FK-consistent JOIN confirmation, subquery depth limits, type checking of arithmetic operations, a function allowlist, and a complexity budget (limits on CROSS JOINs, recursive CTEs, and the number of referenced relations) (the full list is in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_method_details}). The number of checks is automatically tallied from the implementation (the \texttt{check\_*} functions called by \texttt{validate\_sql()}) and always matches \texttt{safety.n\_sqlguard\_checks} in \texttt{paper\_data.json}. Note that prohibition of SELECT * and independent verification of UNION/INTERSECT are not included in the current implementation (column references are verified within the scope of the allowed-column check).

The value of SQLGuard lies not in accuracy improvement but in guaranteeing pre-execution safety and consistency (see Section~\ref{sec:injection_safety}).

\subsection{Hybrid Reranker}

GPT-5.5 is used for reranking SQL candidates. A cross-encoder (local, $<$50\,ms) is used for few-shot example selection, and GPT-5.5 (API) is used for scoring SQL candidates and schema, forming a hybrid architecture.

The reranker compares multiple SQL candidates and selects the best one. For Medium to Hard class queries it is expected to pick out the correct candidate, whereas in the VH class where all candidates are partially wrong, selection becomes harder.

An English/Japanese model comparison of the cross-encoder used for few-shot example selection (on 20 VH queries the Japanese model is $-$4.4\,pp versus ms-marco; the difference is computed from unrounded values) is given in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_method_details}.

\subsection{Execution Feedback and Repair Loop}
\label{sec:repair}

Generated SQL candidates are scored with execution (Section~\ref{sec:nbest}); that is, passing SQLGuard verification, successful execution, a non-empty result set, and coverage of expected conditions are reflected in candidate selection, so candidates causing runtime errors are eliminated at the $n$-best selection stage. Separately, the API service layer implements a repair loop that feeds error messages back to the LLM (GPT-5.5) for up to three repair attempts when SQL execution errors or result-validity problems are detected. However, all evaluations reported in this paper (ablation, independent evaluation, transfer, CTE) were conducted without the repair loop, using only $n$-best execution scoring. All accuracy and syntax-validity values in this paper are therefore values without the repair loop.
\subsection{Database and Evaluation Design}
\label{sec:evaluation}
\subsubsection{Validation Database}

To validate the proposed method, we constructed a validation RDB that takes L1$_2$-type intermetallic compounds as its subject while representing a normalized schema common to inorganic materials databases in general. Beyond L1$_2$ as a representative example, concepts universal to materials RDBs---composition, structure, thermodynamic stability, electronic structure, mechanical properties, surface properties---are arranged into 31 normalized tables and one view (ER diagram in Figure~\ref{fig:er} of Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_method_details}). The configuration, with \texttt{material\_entry} as the central entity connected to peripheral tables via FK relationships, follows the same design pattern as inorganic materials databases such as OQMD, Materials Project, and AFLOW. Key statistics (31 tables, 1 view, \texttt{material\_entry} 1{,}559, \texttt{composition} 3{,}029, \texttt{calculated\_property} 4{,}410, five prototypes) are given in Table~\ref{tab:db_stats} of Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_method_details}.

The public OQMD data model is a flat layout represented by a few wide entry-centric tables, and generic column names such as \texttt{delta\_e} introduce ambiguity for a natural language interface. The correspondence between the flat layout and our normalized schema is shown in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_method_details} (Table~\ref{tab:schema_comparison}).

Formation enthalpy is computed with a multi-step common table expression (CTE) as the difference between pure-element reference energies and the compound total energy. To ensure data reliability, OQMD is used for the pure-element reference energies, while other properties are program-generated synthetic data. Evaluation SQL requires on average 3.4 and at most 6 table joins plus multi-step CTEs; the design defines database complexity as an explicit schema and concentrates complex tasks such as join handling on the pipeline side. The schema transfer experiments (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_eval}) show that the same pipeline can also handle an OQMD-flavored flat layout, an obfuscated schema, and a Materials Project-flavored schema with small accuracy loss.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Queries}
\label{sec:query_design}

We used 100 author-designed queries (Easy 20 / Medium 30 / Hard 30 / Very Hard 20; Table~\ref{tab:query_design} in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_method_details}). Each query is annotated with gold SQL, expected execution results (JSON), required tables, and JOIN paths. Because no standard Text-to-SQL benchmark for inorganic materials RDBs currently exists, we constructed evaluation queries based on the database schema structure and the typical information needs of materials researchers. Queries were designed according to the following principles:
(1) simulate questions arising in real materials research (e.g., searching for stable phases, composition--property correlations);
(2) difficulty is classified into four tiers by a unified complexity score computed from the gold SQL ($3$ per required table + $1$ per WHERE/HAVING condition + $2$ per GROUP BY + $3$ per EXISTS subquery + $3$ per derived subquery; Easy $<8$, Medium $8$--$11$, Hard $12$--$16$, Very Hard (hereafter VH) $\geq 17$. The thresholds 8/12/17 were not derived mechanically from the distribution but set in advance by the authors considering tier size balance (20/30/30/20));
(3) balanced coverage across all categories (A--H);
(4) five CTE multi-step calculation queries are included to evaluate advanced analysis patterns.

The queries intentionally include ambiguous expressions such as ``stable'' and ``large energy difference'' that cannot be interpreted uniquely in natural language. In this study, the operational definition of ``stable'' is $E_\text{hull} \leq 0.001$~eV/atom (metastable: $0.001 < E_\text{hull} \leq 0.05$; unstable: $E_\text{hull} > 0.05$), and the gold SQL, the dictionary, and the few-shot examples all follow this definition. The \texttt{phase\_stability.is\_stable} column in the validation DB is likewise a derived label mechanically obtained from this definition (equivalent to $E_\text{hull} \leq 0.001$) and contains no manual labels. Interpretation of ambiguous expressions depends on the mappings defined by the dictionary and few-shot examples, and an interface that concretizes the question could bring more VH queries closer to Easy. A limitation of author-designed queries is that the query distribution may differ from actual user usage patterns; a user study remains a future validation task.


\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The primary metric is row-level execution recall. The execution results of the generated SQL and the gold SQL are projected onto their common columns and computed as:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Recall}(q) = \frac{|R_\text{gen}^{C} \cap R_\text{gold}^{C}|}{|R_\text{gold}^{C}|}
  \label{eq:column_aware}
\end{equation}
where $C = \text{cols}(R_\text{gen}) \cap \text{cols}(R_\text{gold})$ is the common column set and $R^C$ is the projection onto it. The \texttt{accuracy} field in the legacy evaluation JSON denotes this Recall. Because Recall alone does not penalize over-retrieval, we also report precision $\mathrm{Precision}=|R_\text{gen}^{C}\cap R_\text{gold}^{C}|/|R_\text{gen}^{C}|$ and F1. Row matching is set-based on string-normalized values, ignoring row order and multiplicity. Note that because Recall is defined via projection onto common columns, it can be 1.0 even when the generated SQL returns only part of the correct columns (the reason Precision and F1 are also reported). The scoring protocol has two further lenient aspects: (a)~when there is no column-name overlap at all, matching falls back to column positions; (b)~for queries whose gold SQL returns 0 rows, the generated SQL also returning 0 rows scores 1.0 (0-row agreement is not positive evidence of correctness). The impact of these leniencies is quantified per scoring policy by the bundled audit script \texttt{scripts/audit\_scoring.py} (\texttt{evaluation/metrics\_strict.py}) and reported in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_scoring_audit}. This paper distinguishes two exact-match metrics: (1)~full-column tuple exact match (EM; no projection onto common columns; all columns and all rows of generated and expected results match, order-insensitively), and (2)~Exact Result-Set Match, which is 1 only when Precision=Recall=1 on the common-column projection. Accordingly, ``Recall=100\%'' is not equated with ``all queries perfectly correct.'' During evaluation, LIMIT 10{,}000 is applied to both gold and generated SQL.

The full workflow from data preparation through DB construction, evaluation execution, statistical analysis, figure generation, and numerical consistency verification, together with the scripts and input/output files corresponding to each step, is described in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_workflow}.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Ablation Study}\label{sec:ablation}

To identify which types of queries each component is effective for, we conducted an ablation study under seven conditions (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Note that the VH tier classification is based on the structural complexity of the gold SQL and does not necessarily coincide with the observed difficulty for the LLM (in the independently designed evaluation, VH 72.8\% $>$ Hard 58.5\%; see Limitation (5)). Each condition disables one component and measures the primary metric, execution recall, over all 100 queries. In total, 3{,}500 evaluations (7 conditions $\times$ 100 queries $\times$ 5 runs) were performed. Rather than focusing only on overall accuracy, we investigate the impact of each component on materials-domain-specific query patterns (multi-element conditions, compound property searches, derived-quantity calculations, etc.).

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

We analyze the accuracy sensitivity to parameter changes for the components that showed statistical significance in the ablation study.

\subsubsection{Effect of the Number of Few-shot Examples}
\label{sec:fewshot_sensitivity}

We varied the number of retrieved few-shot examples $k$ over 1, 3, 5, 10, and 15 (Table~\ref{tab:fewshot_k}, Figure~\ref{fig:fewshot_k}; Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_results_details}). Increasing $k$ from 1 to 3 improves the overall score by $+$2.0\,pp (VH tier $+$5.1\,pp), and $k=3$ is the peak both overall (85.6\%) and for VH (55.3\%). Further increases do not help; at $k=15$ VH drops by $-$8.9\,pp relative to $k=3$, an overfitting-like phenomenon in which excessive examples scatter the LLM's attention. Latency is nearly constant in $k$ (24.6--28.9\,s), so from an accuracy standpoint we recommend $k=3$ as the default.

\subsubsection{Effect of Domain Dictionary Size}
\label{sec:dict_sensitivity}

We simultaneously reduced the schema mapping dictionary (61 entries) and the term dictionary to five levels---full / 50\% / 25\% / 10\% / 0\%---and re-evaluated the 100 queries in a single run under the same protocol as the main ablation (Table~\ref{tab:dict_size}, Figure~\ref{fig:dict_size}; Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_results_details}). The full value of 84.7\% is consistent with full in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} (85.2$\pm$0.9\%), and the difference between 81.4\% at 0\% and 80.4\% for no\_dict is within run-to-run variation. The impact is most pronounced in the VH tier, with a drop of $-$5.2\,pp from full to 0\% ($-$3.3\,pp overall). The Easy/Medium tiers stay at 100\%/96.7\% regardless of the dictionary, showing that the dictionary's benefit is concentrated in the harder tiers requiring translation from domain terms to SQL conditions. The non-monotonic variation in the VH tier (e.g., 55.3\% at the 50\% level) is within single-run LLM nondeterminism.

\subsubsection{Effect of LLM Model}
\label{sec:model_comparison}

The same 100 queries were evaluated with GPT-5.5 and GPT-4o (Table~\ref{tab:model_comp}, Figure~\ref{fig:model_comp}; Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_results_details}). GPT-4o is fast with a latency of 4.0\,s (about 1/7 of GPT-5.5), and its overall 85.5\% is comparable to GPT-5.5 full (85.2$\pm$0.9\%) (single run). By tier, the differences are small trade-offs: Medium $-$3.4\,pp, Hard $+$4.2\,pp, VH $+$0.4\,pp. This suggests that when the pipeline's domain-adaptation layers (dictionary, few-shot, FK-graph constraint) are functioning, a lighter model can achieve comparable accuracy at lower cost.

\subsection{Multi-axis Evaluation Metrics}

\label{sec:multiaxis}

Because evaluation based only on execution accuracy (recall) is insufficient, we computed several complementary metrics (all metrics by tier in Table~\ref{tab:multiaxis} and Figure~\ref{fig:radar} of Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_results_details}).

Syntax validity and execution success rate were 100\% in all tiers. This is an observation under the full condition and does not mean SQLGuard guarantees safety for arbitrary inputs. This 100\% was achieved without the repair loop (Section~\ref{sec:repair}) and is the combined effect of SQLGuard verification and $n$-best execution scoring (eliminating candidates that fail execution at the selection stage). Full-column tuple exact match (EM) is low at 25\%, mainly due to added or reordered SELECT columns. Exact Result-Set Match, where Precision=Recall=1 on the common-column projection (definition in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}), was 71\% (71/100 queries, the same single run as the multi-axis evaluation). Recall was 85.6\%, indicating that correct data content is returned. The JOIN match rate is 93.2\% overall, indicating the FK-graph constraint works toward suppressing JOIN-path errors. The 73.5\% JOIN match rate in the VH tier shows that even with the greedy shortest-path connection constraint, complex queries involving five or more joined tables sometimes cannot be resolved to the exact path.
\subsection{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error_analysis}

We analyze the main failure modes from the ablation study results.

\subsubsection{Error Patterns by Ablation Condition}

The main error metrics for each ablation condition are shown in Table~\ref{tab:error_analysis} of Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_results_details}. Under the full condition there were zero syntax errors, execution errors, and table hallucinations, and five queries contained JOINs not based on FK relationships (one under no\_graph, six under no\_dict, and four execution errors under no\_nbest). That execution errors remain only when $n$-best execution scoring is disabled (no\_nbest) demonstrates the contribution of the mechanism that eliminates failing candidates at the selection stage.

In the failure-mode classification of the 8 queries (out of 20 VH queries) with recall below 0.8 in the saved full-condition run (the multi-axis evaluation run), missing SELECT/JOIN of requested property columns (elastic-property pivots, etc.) was most frequent (4), followed by mismatch or absence of the derived composite score expression required by the gold (weighted composite scores) (3), and absence of a derived classification column (CASE-expression stability classification) (1); failures attributable to incorrect JOIN paths were zero. In the error-type distribution of representative failures (15 queries with recall below 0.8), incorrect column selection is the main factor. Details (Table~\ref{tab:vhard_failure}, Figure~\ref{fig:error_dist}, including the distinction of populations and definitions) are given in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_results_details}. These results suggest that future improvement should focus on SELECT-clause generation including derived quantities and composite-score definitions (extended few-shot prompting, chain-of-thought reasoning).

\subsection{SQL Injection and Safety}

\label{sec:injection_safety}

We verified SQLGuard's safety functions using seven adversarial inputs (E01--E05: DROP/DELETE/INSERT etc.; F01--F02: direct SQL input) (Section~\ref{sec:sqlguard}). All inputs were handled safely by SQLGuard (Table~\ref{tab:injection_results}).
E01--E05 were blocked by forbidden-keyword detection, multi-statement detection, the table allowlist, and so on. F01 is a case where a SELECT statement was input directly; LIMIT is automatically added (it is not blocked). F02 is blocked by DML detection. These seven cases can be re-verified deterministically with \texttt{scripts/verify\_safety\_cases.py}, and the reproduction script records the outcome in \texttt{paper\_data.json}. In the ablation, the no\_guard condition changed execution recall by $+$0.8\,pp (no significance judgment due to insufficient power), but the purpose of the safety layer is pre-execution elimination of dangerous SQL, not improvement of mean recall.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Schema Barriers in Inorganic Materials RDBs and the Significance of NL Access}

Our results reconfirm that the main barrier to data utilization in inorganic materials RDBs is not SQL grammar itself but understanding of the normalized schema structure. Both no\_fewshot (79.9\%) and no\_dict (80.4\%) fall well below full (85.2\%) (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), dropping to 44.3\% and 40.0\% respectively in the VH tier. Combining few-shot examples and the materials domain dictionary restores accuracy to 85.2\%, with pronounced gains on complex queries rich in domain-specific vocabulary. The 100\% syntax validity and execution success (Table~\ref{tab:multiaxis}) and the 93.2\% JOIN match rate show that researchers unfamiliar with SQL can use the database safely in natural language. As for schema evolution, the FK-metadata-based design means table additions and FK changes are reflected automatically, and updating the dictionary and few-shot examples can be routinized into standard procedures.

\subsection{Component-wise Contributions}
\label{sec:reranker_discussion}

\paragraph*{Few-shot examples}
Removing few-shot examples caused the largest accuracy drop ($-$5.4\,pp, $p=0.001$; VH tier $-$9.9\,pp, Hard tier $-$6.7\,pp, Medium tier $-$4.5\,pp; Table~\ref{tab:ablation-detail}). The more complex the query, the greater the dependence on few-shot examples.

\paragraph*{Materials domain dictionary}
Removing the dictionary caused an overall drop of $-$4.9\,pp ($p=0.029$). The effect is tier-dependent: no degradation in the Easy/Medium tiers, but $-$6.7\,pp in Hard and $-$14.3\,pp in VH (Table~\ref{tab:ablation-detail}). Easy/Medium queries consist of common expressions for which the LLM can infer SQL conditions on its own, whereas VH queries simultaneously contain multiple domain terms (e.g., ``$\gamma'$ phase'' $\to$ \texttt{structure.prototype = 'L12'}), making correct condition generation difficult without the dictionary.

\paragraph*{Reranker}
Removing the reranker caused a $-$5.1\,pp drop (uncorrected $p=0.016$), with the effect spread broadly across the Medium ($-$6.7\,pp), Hard ($-$5.7\,pp), and VH ($-$7.2\,pp) tiers (Table~\ref{tab:ablation-detail}). A separate standalone A/B evaluation of the reranker (84 queries, both arms regenerated under identical conditions in a single run) also confirmed the contribution of candidate selection itself: 86.4\% with versus 81.5\% without ($+$4.9\,pp; difference computed from unrounded values).

\paragraph*{SQLGuard, $n$-best, and greedy shortest-path connection}
SQLGuard ($+$0.8\,pp, 95\%CI $[-0.2, +2.1]$\,pp), $n$-best ($-$0.8\,pp, $p=0.217$, 95\%CI $[-2.2, +0.6]$\,pp), and greedy shortest-path connection ($+$0.0\,pp, 95\%CI $[-0.6, +0.6]$\,pp) showed no clear effect on raw execution accuracy. However, SQLGuard and greedy shortest-path connection have few non-tied pairs (9 and 6, respectively) and thus insufficient power, so no significance judgment is made (the daggers in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}); these results are not evidence of no effect or equivalence, nor do they imply the components are ``meaningless.'' SQLGuard's main role is as a defensive layer for safety, eliminating SQL injection and non-SELECT statements before execution (the 100\% syntax validity is not interpreted as SQLGuard's sole causal effect). $n$-best = 1 largely preserves accuracy while cutting latency from 29.4\,s to 7.4\,s (Table~\ref{tab:latency}), a valid choice when cost efficiency is the priority.

Greedy shortest-path connection did not change execution accuracy. Queries containing non-FK-based JOINs were few in this saved run---one under no\_graph and five under full (Table~\ref{tab:error_analysis})---and its main role is guaranteeing JOIN-path soundness. One reason no overall accuracy difference appears is that our validation DB, at 31 tables, is small enough for the entire schema to fit in the prompt, consistent with recent observations that schema linking's accuracy contribution materializes only at large schema scales~\cite{maamari2024schema}. That is, no\_graph $\pm$0.0\,pp is an observation dependent on schema scale rather than a failure of the mechanism, and because the design is based on FK introspection, accuracy benefits are also expected to become evident when applied to larger normalized RDBs (Materials Project~\cite{jain2013mp}, AFLOW~\cite{curtarolo2012aflow}).
\subsection{Comparison with Related Systems}

Existing Text-to-SQL methods target general domains and not normalized RDBs in materials science, while materials-domain NL access methods use APIs or RDF/SPARQL and do not support SQL generation against existing RDBs. This study fills the gap with the combination of a normalized RDB, materials domain knowledge, and FK-graph search (comparison table in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_discussion_details}, Table~\ref{tab:related_comparison}).

The technically closest work, SchemaGraphSQL~\cite{safdarian2026schemagraphsql}, differs from ours in that (1)~we include term translation via a materials domain dictionary ($-$4.9\,pp when removed, $p=0.029$), (2)~our JOIN search is a greedy shortest-path connection heuristic on the FK graph (minimality not guaranteed) rather than edge-weight optimization, and (3)~we include AST-based SQL safety verification (SQLGuard).

\paragraph*{Interaction of contributions}
The ablation removes one component at a time, and contributions are not strictly additive due to interactions. Direct comparison with other methods (DIN-SQL, DAIL-SQL, etc.) on the same DB was not performed (see Limitations). For qualitative SQL examples of LLM-only / schema-only configurations, see Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_sql_examples}.

\subsection{Handling Multi-step Calculation Queries}
\label{sec:multistep_query}

The representative example ``compute the formation enthalpy of Ni$_3$Al using pure-element references'' requires retrieval from three tables and conversion into a CTE (common table expression) computing $\Delta H_\mathrm{f} = E_\mathrm{total} - \sum_i x_i E_\mathrm{ref}(i)$ ($E_\mathrm{total}$ is the compound total energy, $E_\mathrm{ref}$ the pure-element reference energy). Our pipeline supports both simple queries via the \texttt{formation\_enthalpy} view and explicit CTE computation via few-shot examples. Conventional benchmarks (Spider~\cite{yu2018spider}, BIRD~\cite{li2024bird}) do not include derived-quantity calculations essential to scientific computing, so the ability to direct such computations in natural language greatly increases practical utility in materials informatics.
\subsection{Evaluation with Independently Designed Queries}
\label{sec:independent_eval}

To test for self-reference bias, we re-evaluated accuracy on 100 queries independently designed by co-author Chie Suematsu, who was not involved in the pipeline implementation. The difficulty distribution is Easy 18 / Medium 34 / Hard 34 / Very Hard 14, spanning ten categories from basic retrieval to materials design and statistics (category breakdown in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_expert_queries}). Scoring used the same execution-recall metric and difficulty classification as the ablation.

Overall execution accuracy was 72.7\% (Easy 83.3\% / Medium 81.3\% / Hard 58.5\% / Very Hard 72.8\%), 12.5\,pp lower than the author-designed set (85.2\%; Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). This gap stems mainly from (1)~a table-coverage bias in which independent queries reference peripheral tables such as \texttt{elastic\_tensor} and \texttt{magnetic\_property} where few-shot examples are sparse (primary factor), (2)~few-shot examples optimized for the fixed patterns of author-designed queries (explicit ``L1$_2$''), and (3)~vocabulary diversity including more implicit condition specification. In this set, 17 of the 100 gold SQL queries return 0 rows, and 10 of them scored 1.0 because the generated SQL also returned 0 rows (leniency (b) of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). The sensitivity value excluding gold-0-row queries from the denominator is 75.6\% ($n=83$) (Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_scoring_audit}).

Syntax validity (100.0\%) is consistent across both sets. The VH tier (72.8\%) being higher than the Hard tier (58.5\%) is because many VH queries contain short CTE patterns covered by few-shot examples, whereas some Hard queries reference peripheral tables without example support (details in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_expert_queries}).

\subsection{Handling Cross-column Comparison Queries}
\label{sec:column_comparison}

Cross-column comparisons of the form ``value A is smaller than value B'' (\texttt{WHERE col\_a > col\_b}) are frequent in materials research but harder for LLMs to generate correctly than constant comparisons. We expanded the few-shot example pool to 45 examples to cover three patterns: intra-table comparison, inter-table (via JOIN) comparison, and comparison using aggregate functions (GROUP BY--HAVING) (examples in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_discussion_details}).

\subsection{RDB Schema Design and Text-to-SQL Cost}
\label{sec:schema_design_cost}

An insight from this study is that the table structure of the RDB itself is an upstream factor determining Text-to-SQL accuracy. Schema design strategies---hiding multi-step JOINs behind views (our \texttt{formation\_enthalpy} view), query-frequency-based denormalization, self-descriptive column names (\texttt{energy} $\to$ \texttt{formation\_energy\_per\_atom}), and explicit FK constraint definitions---can raise NL-interface compatibility from the design stage (details in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_discussion_details}). This perspective applies generally to inorganic materials RDBs with normalized schemas such as OQMD, Materials Project, and AFLOW.

\subsection{Evaluation of CTE Multi-step Calculations}
\label{sec:cte_eval}

Taking formation-enthalpy calculation as the example, five Very Hard queries were replaced by CTE queries and evaluated (category composition and per-condition accuracy table in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_discussion_details}, Table~\ref{tab:cte_results}). The full condition scores 96.0\%; removing few-shot examples drops it to 84.0\% and removing the dictionary to 92.0\%, whereas removing the reranker leaves 96.0\% and removing SQLGuard or the graph JOIN constraint maintains 100\% (removing $n$-best gives 88.0\%), so few-shot examples and the dictionary are the main contributors to CTE syntax generation.

To quantify the applicability limits, we evaluated 15 queries in total, adding 10 new patterns (window functions, two-stage aggregation, three-stage CTEs, etc.). The five known patterns maintained 100\%, but the ten novel zero-shot patterns reached only 40.3\% (60.2\% overall): the scope of multi-step calculation support is controlled by few-shot pattern coverage. Conversely, extension to new patterns can be achieved by adding roughly one example per pattern, with no model retraining.

\subsection{Extensibility to Similar Inorganic Materials RDBs}
\label{sec:extensibility}

The design principles of this study are potentially applicable beyond the validation DB. Greedy shortest-path connection based on FK introspection can automatically build the graph for RDBs with explicit FK constraints, and the four-category dictionary structure (prototype aliases, element mappings, stability terms, property terms) also applies to inorganic materials RDBs in general. However, schema-specific information remains in the semantics of required tables and the mapping of condition categories, and few-shot examples depend on the validation DB schema, so applying the pipeline to a different RDB requires new example design by domain experts. We make no claim of generalization performance to RDBs with fundamentally different semantics or much larger scale.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

The main limitations are the following eleven (details for each in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_limitations}).
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Single-database validation: the main evaluation is based on a validation DB centered on L1$_2$ with five prototypes (31 tables, 1 view, 1{,}559 entries, roughly 19{,}973 total rows across all tables; only pure-element reference energies are OQMD-derived), and unmodified generalization to RDBs with fundamentally different semantics or scale has not been confirmed.
  \item Limits of the 31-table, 1-view configuration: the schema is smaller than real materials RDBs and does not cover the diversity of attribute names or different normalization policies.
  \item Scope of statistical tests: query-level bootstrap 95\%CIs are attached to the overall $\Delta$ (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), but five repetitions are insufficient for tier-level interval estimation (especially VH, $n=20$), and tier-level $\Delta$ remains exploratory. no\_guard and no\_graph also have few non-tied pairs and insufficient test power.
  \item Query coverage: the 100 author-designed queries do not exhaustively cover the diverse patterns of real usage.
  \item Validity of the difficulty score: the score measures the structural complexity of the gold SQL and does not always correspond monotonically to observed difficulty for the LLM (in the independent set, VH 72.8\% $>$ Hard 58.5\%). Refinement incorporating few-shot coverage is future work.
  \item Gold SQL verification: no complete independent third-party verification by external researchers has been performed.
  \item Dictionary coverage: absorption of expression variants (e.g., OR expansion of prototype specifications across the \texttt{prototype} and \texttt{strukturbericht} columns) is guaranteed only for aliases registered in the dictionary; unregistered synonyms and abbreviations can cause retrieval misses.
  \item Ambiguous natural language expressions: interpretations of ``stable'' and the like are not unique, leaving room for a complementary dialogue interface that concretizes the question (examples in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_ambiguous}).
  \item Language dependence: the evaluation queries are Japanese; application to English and other languages requires preparing dictionaries and few-shot examples.
  \item Baselines: no direct comparison with other methods (DIN-SQL, DAIL-SQL, etc.) on the same DB was performed.
  \item Leniency of the scoring protocol: the primary execution-recall metric is a lenient definition including common-column projection, positional fallback, and full credit for 0-row agreement (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}); auditing revealed overestimation cases in some derived-computation queries via identifier-column-only matches (Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_scoring_audit}). Values under stricter scoring policies are also reported there.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Future Prospects}

Future work includes: (1)~validation on inorganic materials RDBs with different schemas such as Materials Project and AFLOW; (2)~validation on data far exceeding the current 1{,}559 entries (roughly 20,000 rows), at the scale of tens of thousands of entries; (3)~extending the CTE evaluation to more diverse derived-quantity patterns (phase-stability diagrams, elastic anisotropy metrics, etc.); (4)~integrating T2SQL into AI4S agents via the MCP protocol or function calling; (5)~a two-stage flow combined with CALPHAD free-energy calculations; (6)~routine use of multi-axis evaluation such as SELECT-column precision, JOIN match rate, and Top-$k$ accuracy; (7)~a question-concretization interface that resolves ambiguous expressions through dialogue.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This study presented the design principles of a Text-to-SQL pipeline realizing natural language access to inorganic materials RDBs and demonstrated its effectiveness using a 31-table L1$_2$-prototype RDB.

From the ablation study of 7 conditions $\times$ 100 queries $\times$ 5 runs (3{,}500 evaluations in total), few-shot examples ($-$5.4\,pp, $p=0.001$, significant after Holm correction) proved essential for materials-specific query patterns, and the materials domain dictionary ($-$4.9\,pp, $p=0.029$) and the reranker ($-$5.1\,pp, $p=0.016$) also make contributions significant before correction. Improvements were pronounced for questions involving multi-element conditions, compound property searches, and domain terminology. In contrast, the FK-graph JOIN constraint did not change overall accuracy at this schema scale ($+$0.0\,pp, 95\%CI $[-0.6, +0.6]$\,pp). JOIN-path errors are few, and the main remaining failure mode is missing requested property columns or derived score expressions (Section~\ref{sec:error_analysis}).

The multi-axis evaluation---execution recall 85.6\%, precision 84.7\%, F1 79.3\%, syntax validity 100\%, JOIN match rate 93.2\%---assessed result-set coverage, over-retrieval, SQL syntax, and JOIN paths separately. SQLGuard functioned as a safety layer eliminating non-SELECT statements and the like before execution, though the 100\% syntax validity is a value on this evaluation set, not a general safety guarantee. The generalization tests yielded execution recall of A~95.0\%, B~95.0\%, C~90.0\%, and D~100.0\%, but A is a domain expansion on the same schema and D is a lightweight adaptation using MP-specific few-shot examples, so these are not collectively interpreted as evidence of zero adaptation or complete schema independence. On the 15 CTE multi-step calculation patterns including formation-enthalpy calculation from pure-element references, the five known patterns with few-shot examples achieved 100\% and the ten novel patterns without examples 40.3\% (60.2\% overall), demonstrating both the feasibility of derived calculations and that their coverage is determined by few-shot pattern coverage. Together with the additional validation on 100 independently designed queries (execution recall 72.7\%), a practical foundation is in place for materials researchers who do not write SQL to perform cross-table exploration and engineering-oriented inquiry.

These results reconfirm that the main barrier to data utilization in inorganic materials RDBs lies not in SQL grammar itself but in understanding normalized schema structure. When placing LLMs at the front line of scientific data, combining constraint mechanisms such as the schema graph, domain dictionary, SQLGuard, and reranker enables safe use while correctly handling the relational structure of scientific data. The pipeline can add a natural language interface to existing materials RDB infrastructure (MInt~\cite{minamoto2020mint}, pinax~\cite{minamoto2026pinax}, etc.) without forcing data conversion or migration, and in the future---through question-concretization interfaces and coupling with CALPHAD calculations---is expected to become a component of autonomous materials exploration loops.

\section{Generated SQL Examples}
\label{sec:sup_sql_examples}

We compare generated SQL examples for representative queries across three method configurations (LLM only, full schema, and our method). This complements the quantitative comparison in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} by concretely showing the qualitative differences in output SQL under each configuration.

\subsection{``List stable L1$_2$ compounds containing Ni''}

\paragraph{LLM only (no schema):}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize,breaklines=true]
SELECT formula, stability
FROM materials
WHERE element = 'Ni'
  AND structure_type = 'L12'
  AND stability = 'stable';
-- Table hallucination: materials, stability, etc. do not exist
-- Not executable (ERROR: relation "materials" does not exist)
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Full schema (schema in prompt, no few-shot examples):}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize,breaklines=true]
SELECT m.formula, ps.is_stable
FROM material_entry m
  JOIN composition c ON c.entry_id = m.entry_id
  JOIN structure s ON s.entry_id = m.entry_id
  JOIN phase_stability ps ON ps.entry_id = m.entry_id
WHERE c.element = 'Ni'
  AND s.strukturbericht = 'L12'
  AND ps.is_stable = true;
-- Executable but incomplete (is_stable is a derived column
-- for E_hull<=0.001, equivalent to our stability condition):
-- (1) no prototype condition (misses L12 absent from strukturbericht)
-- (2) no DISTINCT/LIMIT
-- (3) does not select practically useful columns such as lattice_a
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Our method (schema graph + few-shot + dictionary):}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize,breaklines=true]
SELECT DISTINCT m.entry_id, m.formula,
  s.prototype, s.lattice_a, s.space_group
FROM material_entry m
  JOIN composition c ON c.entry_id = m.entry_id
  JOIN structure s ON s.entry_id = m.entry_id
  JOIN phase_stability ps ON ps.entry_id = m.entry_id
WHERE (s.prototype = 'L12'
    OR s.strukturbericht = 'L12')
  AND c.element = 'Ni'
  AND ps.energy_above_hull <= 0.001
LIMIT 10000;
-- Schema-graph traversal requires only three JOINs
-- No table hallucination; includes DISTINCT and LIMIT
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{``List L1$_2$ compounds containing both Ni and Al''}

\paragraph{LLM only (no schema):}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize,breaklines=true]
SELECT * FROM compounds
WHERE elements LIKE '%Ni%' AND elements LIKE '%Al%'
  AND crystal_structure = 'L12';
-- Table hallucination: compounds and the elements column do not exist
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Full schema (no few-shot examples):}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize,breaklines=true]
SELECT m.entry_id, m.formula
FROM material_entry m
  JOIN composition c ON c.entry_id = m.entry_id
  JOIN structure s ON s.entry_id = m.entry_id
WHERE c.element = 'Ni' AND c.element = 'Al'
  AND s.prototype = 'L12';
-- Fatal: no single row can satisfy both conditions -> always 0 rows
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Our method (EXISTS subqueries):}
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize,breaklines=true]
SELECT DISTINCT m.entry_id, m.formula
FROM material_entry m
  JOIN structure s ON s.entry_id = m.entry_id
WHERE (s.prototype = 'L12'
    OR s.strukturbericht = 'L12')
AND EXISTS (
  SELECT 1 FROM composition
  WHERE entry_id = m.entry_id AND element = 'Ni')
AND EXISTS (
  SELECT 1 FROM composition
  WHERE entry_id = m.entry_id AND element = 'Al')
LIMIT 10000;
-- Exact multi-element AND via EXISTS subqueries
\end{lstlisting}

The structured prompt consists of four components:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item System message: task description + schema YAML
    (table names, column names, types, FK relationships)
  \item Few-shot examples: top-$k$ similar NL--SQL pairs
    selected by TF-IDF + cross-encoder
  \item User message: the natural language query
  \item Constraints: ``generate SELECT statements only,''
    ``include LIMIT 10000,''
    ``use only tables listed in the schema''
\end{enumerate}

The GPT-5 series API does not support the temperature parameter, and our implementation does not send it. As a result, outputs may differ for identical inputs due to the LLM's internal state. All ablation results in this paper are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over five independent runs, with statistical significance confirmed by query-level paired sign-flip tests and bootstrap confidence intervals (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\subsection{Details of the Greedy Shortest-Path JOIN Connector}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
  \caption{Greedy shortest-path JOIN heuristic connecting required tables.}
  \label{alg:join_connector}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require FK relationship graph $G = (V, E)$, required table set $R$
    \Ensure JOIN path list $P$
    \State $P \gets \emptyset$; initialize union--find $U$
    \ForAll{$(s, t) \in \binom{R}{2}$}
      \If{$\text{Find}(s) = \text{Find}(t)$}
        \State continue
      \EndIf
      \State $p \gets \text{ShortestPath}(G, s, t)$ \Comment{BFS on the FK graph}
      \If{$p \neq \emptyset$}
        \State $P \gets P \cup \{p\}$
        \ForAll{adjacent node pairs $(u, v) \in p$}
          \State $\text{Union}(u, v)$
        \EndFor
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $P$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For node count $|V| = n$ and required table count $|R| = k$, the algorithm performs $O(k^2)$ BFS traversals. At the 31-table scale ($n = 31$, $k \leq 6$) this completes in milliseconds.

This heuristic aims to connect the required table set via FK paths while suppressing the addition of unrelated tables. However, because it can depend on the processing order of table pairs, it does not guarantee minimal coverage. Since FK relationships are obtained at runtime from PostgreSQL metadata, schema changes are reflected in the graph as long as FKs are correctly defined.

\subsubsection{Graph Construction from PostgreSQL Metadata}

FK relationships are extracted dynamically at runtime from PostgreSQL's \texttt{information\_schema}.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=SQL,caption={Extraction of FK relationships},label=lst:fk_query,numbers=none]
SELECT tc.table_name, kcu.column_name,
       ccu.table_name, ccu.column_name
  FROM information_schema.table_constraints tc
  JOIN information_schema.key_column_usage kcu
    ON tc.constraint_name = kcu.constraint_name
  JOIN information_schema.constraint_column_usage ccu
    ON ccu.constraint_name = tc.constraint_name
 WHERE tc.constraint_type = 'FOREIGN KEY';
\end{lstlisting}

The result is stored as an undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ in NetworkX~\cite{networkx2008}. Each edge retains the FK direction as an attribute used when generating JOIN clauses. Because FK metadata is read at runtime, the system can track schema changes.

\subsubsection{Multi-element AND Queries: EXISTS Subqueries}
\label{sec:multi_element}

A materials-specific challenge arising from the normalization of the composition table is queries such as ``compounds containing both Ni and Al.'' The naive approach \texttt{WHERE c.element = 'Ni' AND c.element = 'Al'} always returns zero rows because no single row satisfies both conditions. The schema graph engine detects when multiple elements are specified and automatically generates EXISTS subqueries:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=SQL,caption={Multi-element AND},label=lst:exists,numbers=none]
SELECT DISTINCT m.entry_id, m.formula
FROM material_entry m
WHERE EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM composition
  WHERE entry_id = m.entry_id AND element = 'Ni')
AND EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM composition
  WHERE entry_id = m.entry_id AND element = 'Al')
LIMIT 10000;
\end{lstlisting}

AND/OR intent is classified by the condition extractor from conjunctions and particles. For OR indicators, an \texttt{IN} clause is used.

\subsubsection{SQL Generation from JOIN Paths}

$P$ is the set of edges contained in the JOIN paths obtained by Algorithm~\ref{alg:join_connector}. Each path in $P$ is converted into an SQL JOIN clause. Table aliases are assigned from a fixed dictionary for all 31 tables (\texttt{material\_entry}$\to$\texttt{m}, \texttt{composition}$\to$\texttt{c}, etc.). When the same table appears multiple times, a numeric suffix produces unique aliases (\texttt{c2}, \texttt{c3}).

\subsection{Condition Extractor}

The condition extractor converts natural language queries into structured condition dictionaries. It uses a YAML-based bilingual (Japanese--English) term dictionary (\texttt{material\_terms.yaml}).

\subsubsection{Dictionary Structure}

The condition extractor uses a bilingual (Japanese--English) term dictionary (\texttt{material\_terms.yaml}) that reflects the table/column structure of the target RDB. That is, it maps natural language expressions to the database schema based on the actual tables and columns of \texttt{material\_entry}, \texttt{structure}, \texttt{composition}, \texttt{phase\_stability}, and so on.

The dictionary covers four categories:
(1)~prototype aliases (e.g., \texttt{L12} $\leftrightarrow$ [L1$_2$, Cu$_3$Au type, $\gamma'$]),
(2)~element mappings (\texttt{Fe} $\leftrightarrow$ [iron]),
(3)~stability terms (``stable'' $\to$ \texttt{energy\_above\_hull} $\leq 0.001$~eV/atom; ``metastable'' $\to$ $0.001 < E_\text{hull} \leq 0.05$),
(4)~property terms (``lattice constant'' $\to$ \texttt{structure.lattice\_a}, etc.).

Unicode subscripts (U+2080--U+2089) are normalized to ASCII digits before matching. Element symbol recognition uses word-boundary regular expressions to distinguish ``Fe'' from a substring of ``FeCoNi.''

\subsubsection{Chemical Formula Parser and Ambiguity Handling}

Chemical formula tokens are classified into three types:
(1)~exact\_formula (\texttt{Ni3Al} $\to$ \texttt{formula = 'Ni3Al'}),
(2)~formula\_or\_reduced\_formula (``B2 entries of NiAl'' $\to$ \texttt{reduced\_formula = 'AlNi'}),
(3)~contains\_elements (``containing Ni and Al'' $\to$ EXISTS subqueries).
Classification is context-dependent: co-occurrence with expressions such as ``containing'' triggers \texttt{contains\_elements} handling.

\subsubsection{Coverage Score}

The coverage score quantifies the fraction of semantic tokens in the query recognized by the dictionary, the element dictionary, or numeric patterns, producing a value in $[0, 1]$. This metric prevents silent constraint dropping (silent discarding of unrecognized vocabulary). A score $\geq 0.8$ triggers rule-based mode execution; $\geq 0.5$ escalates to LLM mode; $< 0.5$ requests confirmation from the user.

\subsubsection{Intent Classifier}

A rule-based intent classifier sits upstream of SQL generation and classifies queries into five categories. It compares regex match counts of 20 VASP/DFT workflow patterns against 9 DB-specific patterns to pre-filter out-of-scope questions.

\subsection{The 15 SQLGuard Checks}

SQLGuard's static verification comprises 15 checks, namely the following check functions called by \texttt{validate\_sql()} (in one-to-one correspondence with the implementation in \texttt{safety/sql\_validator.py}):

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item Multi-statement detection (\texttt{check\_multiple\_statements})
  \item Forbidden keyword detection: DML/DDL such as DROP/DELETE/INSERT/UPDATE (\texttt{check\_forbidden\_keywords})
  \item SELECT-only statement confirmation (\texttt{check\_select\_only})
  \item Prohibition of dangerous functions (\texttt{check\_disallowed\_functions})
  \item Prohibition of system table references (\texttt{check\_system\_tables})
  \item SELECT-only confirmation of CTE bodies (\texttt{check\_cte\_bodies\_select\_only})
  \item Tautology (SQL injection pattern) detection (\texttt{check\_tautology})
  \item LIMIT clause presence check with automatic addition when missing (\texttt{check\_limit})
  \item Table existence check against the allowed-table list (\texttt{check\_allowed\_tables})
  \item Column existence check against the whitelist after alias resolution; column references in ORDER BY/GROUP BY are also verified by this check (\texttt{check\_allowed\_columns})
  \item FK-consistent JOIN verification (\texttt{check\_join\_validity})
  \item Subquery nesting depth limit (\texttt{check\_subquery\_depth})
  \item Type validity of arithmetic operations (\texttt{check\_column\_type\_safety})
  \item Function allowlist: rejects calls to functions outside the allowed list (\texttt{check\_function\_allowlist})
  \item Complexity budget: limits on CROSS JOINs, recursive CTEs, and the number of referenced relations (\texttt{check\_complexity\_budget})
\end{enumerate}

Prohibition of SELECT *, DISTINCT recommendation on JOINs, and independent verification of UNION/INTERSECT clauses do not exist in the current implementation. Queries containing SELECT * are not rejected (as long as they stay within allowed tables and columns).

\subsubsection{Japanese Cross-Encoder Comparison}

For the cross-encoder used in few-shot example selection, we compared an English model (ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2) and a Japanese model (hotchpotch/japanese-reranker-cross-encoder-xsmall-v1) on 20 Very Hard queries. The English model achieved 55.2\% and the Japanese model 45.3\% (the Japanese model is $-$9.9\,pp relative to ms-marco). This comparison is a standalone measurement, and the absolute values are not directly comparable to the VH tier of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} (5-run mean 54.2\%). Because few-shot examples contain SQL code (English), ms-marco's English scoring capability may contribute to its advantage.


\subsection{Relationship to the Source OQMD-style Data Layout}

\paragraph*{Relationship to the source OQMD-style data layout}

The public OQMD data model can be represented by a few wide, entry-centric tables (e.g., \texttt{entries}, \texttt{element\_ratios}, \texttt{formation\_energies}, \texttt{reference\_states}, and \texttt{elements}). This layout is efficient for accumulating high-throughput data, but stores structurally distinct information (composition, crystal structure, thermodynamic stability, electronic properties) in generic columns such as \texttt{delta\_e} and \texttt{composition\_formula}. For a natural language interface, this causes ambiguity: ``energy'' may refer to formation energy, hull distance, or band gap; ``Ti'' may be a constituent element or the target of a property filter.

\subsection{Handling Cross-column Comparison Queries (Details)}

In addition to multi-step calculations, cross-column comparison is a representative query pattern frequently encountered in materials research. Queries of the form ``find entries where value A is smaller than value B'' require SQL generation with conditional expressions comparing values in different columns of the same row (\texttt{WHERE col\_a > col\_b}), unlike single threshold filtering (\texttt{WHERE col > 0.5}).

Our pipeline provides few-shot examples covering three kinds of cross-column comparison patterns.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Intra-table comparison:
    direct comparison of two columns within the same table.
    Examples: \texttt{formation\_energy\_per\_atom < energy\_above\_hull} (within \texttt{phase\_stability});
    \texttt{bulk\_modulus\_vrh > 2.0 * shear\_modulus\_vrh} (within \texttt{elastic\_tensor}, the Pugh ductility metric).
  \item Inter-table comparison:
    comparing columns from different tables via JOINs.
    Examples: \texttt{thermal\_property.debye\_temperature\_k > magnetic\_property.curie\_temperature\_k};
    total magnetization versus absolute formation energy.
  \item Comparison with aggregate functions:
    threshold evaluation of derived quantities such as electronegativity differences among constituents, combined with GROUP BY--HAVING.
    Example: \texttt{HAVING MAX(electronegativity) - MIN(electronegativity) > 0.5}.
\end{enumerate}

These patterns are harder for LLMs to generate correctly than constant comparisons (\texttt{WHERE col > 0.5}). Inter-table comparisons in particular require accurately identifying, from schema information, which table each compared column belongs to. We expanded the few-shot example pool to 45 examples, mainly by adding cross-column comparison patterns, enabling the LLM to learn such patterns.


\subsection{RDB Schema Design and Text-to-SQL Cost (Details)}

An insight gained through this study is that the table structure of the RDB itself is an upstream factor determining Text-to-SQL accuracy.

Highly normalized schemas require multiple table JOINs for a single query, increasing the complexity of the SQL the LLM must generate. The formation enthalpy calculation in our database (Section~\ref{sec:multistep_query}) required a three-table JOIN of \texttt{phase\_stability}, \texttt{composition}, and \texttt{pure\_element\_reference}, plus CTE-based aggregate calculations. The $-$5.4\,pp accuracy drop without few-shot examples confirms that more complex inter-table join patterns make accurate SQL generation by the LLM harder.

Based on this insight, the following schema design strategies for T2SQL readiness can be considered:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Hiding complexity via views:
    define frequently used multi-step JOIN patterns as views or materialized views, providing the LLM an interface queryable with a single SELECT. The \texttt{formation\_enthalpy} view in this study is an example.
  \item Denormalization based on query frequency:
    provide frequently referenced table groups (e.g., \texttt{composition} and \texttt{element}) as pre-joined tables, reducing the number of JOINs.
  \item Self-descriptive column naming:
    embed table context in column names (e.g., \texttt{energy} $\to$ \texttt{formation\_energy\_per\_atom}), increasing the probability the LLM selects the appropriate column from schema information.
  \item Explicit FK constraint definitions:
    the greedy shortest-path connector of this implementation builds JOIN paths based on FK relationships. It does not guarantee a globally minimal JOIN path, and automatic JOIN resolution becomes difficult in schemas lacking FK constraints. Where T2SQL readiness is a design goal, comprehensive FK constraint definitions are necessary.
\end{enumerate}

In summary, improving the accuracy of Text-to-SQL systems requires considering NL interface compatibility at the database schema design stage, not just pipeline-side improvements. This perspective serves as a design guideline applicable not only to the validation DB used in this study, but generally to inorganic materials RDBs with similar normalized schemas (OQMD, Materials Project, AFLOW, etc.) and to domain-specific RDBs where Text-to-SQL is applied.

\subsection{Details of the Generalization Evaluation Variants}

Variant A---five-prototype cross-prototype queries.
Against the same validation DB as the main evaluation (containing B2/NaCl/NiAs/BiF$_3$; the statistics of Table~\ref{tab:db_stats} are for this DB), we executed 20 evaluation queries of identical design including queries targeting non-L1$_2$ prototypes. The result was 95.0\% (Easy 100\% / Medium 100\% / Hard 100\% / Very Hard 80\%), confirming that prototype separation and the FK graph are robust across crystal prototypes.

Variant B---OQMD-style renaming. We constructed an OQMD-style flat five-table schema in which all table and column names were changed (\texttt{oqmd\_entries}, \texttt{oqmd\_element\_ratios}, \texttt{oqmd\_formation\_energies}, \texttt{oqmd\_reference\_states}, \texttt{oqmd\_elements}). Few-shot examples were presented unchanged from the original schema, and the prompt instructed: ``few-shot examples may come from a different schema; reuse only their SQL patterns.'' The result was 95.0\% overall.

Variant C---identifier obfuscation.
All schema identifiers in the transfer DB were replaced with random English words while preserving FK relationships. Execution recall was 90.0\%. This result shows that a certain level of performance can be maintained even when meaningful identifier names are lost. However, because schema presentation to the LLM, the transfer prompt, and FK relationships are used simultaneously, we do not conclude from this experiment alone that ``FK topology alone is sufficient.''

Variant D---Materials Project-style schema.
We retrieved 27 binary systems (299 entries) from real Materials Project REST data and constructed a new three-table PostgreSQL schema (\texttt{mp\_entries}, \texttt{mp\_elements}, \texttt{mp\_element\_ratios}). For 15 Japanese queries, we provided 8 few-shot examples written for the MP schema and an MP-specific prompt, but the pipeline code itself was unchanged. Execution recall, the conventional primary metric, was 100.0\% overall (100.0\% in every tier). Note, however, that this stored evaluation result recorded Recall as the primary value and does not mean that full result-set matches without over-retrieval were 15/15. The revised evaluation script also stores Precision, F1, and Exact Result-Set Match.

These results show that the pipeline can potentially be applied under conditions departing from the original 31-table, L1$_2$-centric validation environment. However, the grounds for generalization differ per variant. In particular, while D is useful as an example of extension to external real data, it requires MP-specific few-shot examples, so we position it as a demonstration of lightweight adaptation rather than proof of schema independence.

Discovery of hidden schema dependencies.
The transfer tests also served as a verification tool, prompting the discovery and reduction of hard-coding specific to the original schema. However, the current implementation still contains some elements that assume the original schema, such as the mapping table between condition categories and table names, the base table name, and auxiliary semantic JOINs. Therefore, this paper does not claim ``fully schema-independent''; rather, we position the design as one in which using FK metadata reduces the scope of modifications required upon change.


\subsection{Details of Limitations}
\label{sec:sup_limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Single-database validation:
    the main evaluation of this study is based on a validation database centered on L1$_2$ and containing the five prototypes B2/NaCl/NiAs/BiF$_3$ (31 tables, 1 view, 1{,}559 entries in the central table, 19{,}973 total rows across all tables; only pure-element reference energies are OQMD-derived). The generalization evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:transfer_eval}) examined five-prototype cross-prototype queries on the same schema (95.0\%), OQMD-style renaming (95.0\%), obfuscated identifiers (90.0\%), and real Materials Project data with MP-specific few-shot examples (100.0\%), but the conditions are not identical, and unmodified generalization to RDBs with fundamentally different semantics or scale has not been confirmed.
  \item Limits of the 31-table, 1-view configuration:
    this is strictly a validation environment, and the schema size is smaller than real materials RDBs. Being based on one representative schema, it does not cover the diversity of attribute names (synonyms, abbreviations, multiple languages) or different normalization policies. Tacit knowledge that cannot be resolved from schema metadata alone (e.g., the meaning of ``stable'' in a particular materials system) remains.
  \item Scope of statistical testing:
    statistical tests used each query's 5-run mean execution recall as the query value ($n=100$) and applied a two-sided sign-flip (permutation) test with the mean difference as the test statistic to the paired differences between the full condition and each removal condition (pairs with zero difference are effectively excluded; the number of non-tied pairs $n_\mathrm{eff}$ is reported). The table shows uncorrected p-values for the 6 comparisons together with effect sizes (rank-biserial) and query-level bootstrap 95\% CIs, and also notes the Bonferroni threshold ($p<0.0083$). For no\_guard ($n_\mathrm{eff}=9$) and no\_graph ($n_\mathrm{eff}=6$, minimum attainable p-value $=0.03125$), the number of non-tied pairs is small and power is insufficient, so significance is not assessed. Five repetitions are insufficient for stratified confidence interval evaluation (especially VH, $n=20$), and stratified $\Delta$ remains exploratory analysis.
  \item Query coverage:
    the 100 author-designed queries are designed to cover the main retrieval patterns in materials research, but do not exhaustively cover the diverse query patterns encountered in actual use.
  \item Validity of the difficulty score:
    the unified complexity score (Section~\ref{sec:query_design}) is based on the structural complexity of gold SQL and does not always correspond monotonically to observed difficulty. In the 100 independently designed queries, the VH tier (72.8\%) exceeded the Hard tier (58.5\%), and in transfer variant B the VH tier (100.0\%) exceeded the Medium tier (83.3\%). This is because few-shot examples cover some VH patterns (short CTEs, etc.) while there exist Hard queries unaided by few-shot examples; the score measures the structural complexity of SQL rather than ``difficulty for the LLM.'' This is a limitation of the difficulty metric, and refining the score to account for few-shot coverage is future work.
  \item Gold SQL verification:
    gold SQL and expected results were created by the authors; the independently designed 100-query set was cross-checked by co-author Chie Suematsu, but full independent third-party verification by external researchers has not been performed.
  \item Dictionary coverage:
    the 492-term dictionary is specialized for L1$_2$ exploration; extension to Heusler, perovskite, and other systems requires manual registration. Absorption of expression variants (e.g., expanding a prototype specification into an OR condition over both \texttt{prototype} and \texttt{strukturbericht} columns) is guaranteed only for aliases registered in the dictionary; unregistered synonyms and abbreviations can cause retrieval misses. A transition to semi-automatic generation from schema metadata is desirable.
  \item Handling ambiguous natural language expressions:
    the legitimate interpretation of ambiguous expressions such as ``stable'' or ``large energy difference'' is not unique, and word matching or dictionary mappings alone may fail to determine context-dependent comparison targets or thresholds. Seven concrete examples representing missing conditions, contradictions, and out-of-schema references are given in Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_ambiguous}. Combining an interface that concretizes the question (interactively fixing target elements, thresholds, and comparison axes, and pointing out contradictions and omissions) could bring Very Hard queries closer to Easy.
  \item Language dependence:
    the evaluation queries in this study are Japanese, but the core pipeline design (LLM-based SQL generation, schema linking, few-shot retrieval, shortest-path JOIN generation on the FK graph) is language-independent. However, some components are language-dependent, such as the domain dictionaries (bilingual term dictionary and schema mapping dictionary), so applying the pipeline to English or other languages requires preparing the tokenizer, dictionaries, and few-shot examples for the target language (systematic validation with English queries was not performed in this study).
  \item Baseline fairness:
    direct comparison with other methods (DIN-SQL, DAIL-SQL, etc.) on the same database has not been performed.
  \item Leniency of the scoring protocol:
    the primary metric, execution recall, has a lenient definition including projection onto common columns, positional column fallback when column names do not overlap at all, and full credit for agreement of gold zero rows with generated zero rows (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). Values under stricter scoring policies and the results of a manual audit of queries scored full marks on only one matching gold column (including over-credited derived-calculation cases) are given in Table~\ref{tab:scoring_audit} of Supplementary Section~\ref{sec:sup_scoring_audit}. For the independent set, the sensitivity value 75.6\% ($n=83$) excluding the 17 gold zero-row queries (10 of which received full marks via zero-row agreement) is reported alongside.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Examples of Ambiguous and Contradictory Queries}
\label{sec:sup_ambiguous}

Examples of ambiguous or contradictory queries that real users might submit include: (i)~``show L1$_2$-structure ones with the NiAl composition ratio'' (NiAl is a 1:1-composition B2 type, contradicting the 1:3-composition L1$_2$ premise); (ii)~``show stable compounds'' (the criterion---hull distance or the sign of formation enthalpy---is unspecified); (iii)~``stable compounds with formation enthalpy greater than 0.5\,eV'' (stability implies negative formation enthalpy, contradicting the threshold); (iv)~``L1$_2$ compounds with melting point above 2{,}000\,K'' (a question about an attribute absent from the schema); (v)~``show $\gamma$-phase alloys'' (ambiguous between steel's $\gamma$ (fcc) and Ni-base superalloys' $\gamma'$); (vi)~``which element is most common?'' (the aggregation unit---material count or composition fraction---is unclear); (vii)~``compounds with the D0$_{19}$ structure'' (a prototype absent from the validation database). These represent distinct failure modes: missing conditions, contradictions, and out-of-schema references.

\section{Reproduction Workflow: From Data Preparation to Result Interpretation}
\label{sec:sup_workflow}

This section describes the full workflow from data preparation to interpretation of analysis results, in one-to-one correspondence with the scripts bundled in the distribution package. The input/output file names and script names of each step match the implementation exactly. Because the evaluation execution of Step 4 requires an LLM API (\texttt{OPENAI\_API\_KEY}) and is non-deterministic, all numbers in the paper are regenerated deterministically from the evaluation outputs stored in the repository (the inputs of Steps 5--6).

\paragraph{Step 1: Data preparation.}
(a)~Synthetic validation data: \texttt{ingestion/generate\_extended\_data.py} (deterministic with random seed \texttt{random.seed(42)}) generates 1{,}470 compound entries across the five prototypes (L1$_2$/B2/NaCl/NiAs/BiF$_3$) and synthetic data for the 30 tables excluding the pure-element reference table, output as \texttt{db/insert\_data.sql} (the stability label \texttt{is\_stable} is derived mechanically from $E_\text{hull} \leq 0.001$~eV/atom).
(b)~OQMD-derived real data: \texttt{scripts/fetch\_oqmd\_pure\_elements.py} retrieves pure-element ground-state data from the OQMD REST API (the \texttt{formationenergy} endpoint, \texttt{ntypes=1}) and saves it to \texttt{db/pure\_element\_data.json}, and \texttt{scripts/generate\_pure\_element\_sql.py} converts it into \texttt{db/insert\_pure\_elements.sql} (89 pure-element entries). The 1{,}559 rows of the central table \texttt{material\_entry} are 1{,}470 (synthetic) $+$ 89 (OQMD-derived).
(c)~Language resources: \texttt{scripts/build\_mecab\_materials\_dict.py} compiles the custom MeCab dictionary \texttt{llm/mecab\_materials.csv}/\texttt{.dic} from the term dictionaries.

\paragraph{Step 2: Database construction.}
\texttt{docker/docker-compose.yml} starts a PostgreSQL 15 container and automatically loads \texttt{db/extended\_schema.sql} (schema), \texttt{db/insert\_data.sql}, and \texttt{db/insert\_pure\_elements.sql} in this order as initialization scripts (via \texttt{docker-entrypoint-initdb.d}). The generalization evaluation DBs are built by \texttt{scripts/build\_transfer\_db.py} (variant B: OQMD-style renaming, \texttt{db/transfer\_schema.sql}), \texttt{scripts/build\_obfuscated\_transfer\_db.py} (variant C: identifier obfuscation), and \texttt{scripts/prepare\_mp\_transfer.py} plus \texttt{scripts/build\_mp\_transfer\_db.py} (variant D: three tables of real Materials Project data).

\paragraph{Step 3: Evaluation datasets.}
The 100 main evaluation queries are stored in \texttt{evaluation/evaluation\_dataset.jsonl}, gold SQL in \texttt{evaluation/gold\_sql/}, and expected execution results in \texttt{evaluation/expected\_results/}. The 100 independently designed queries are in \texttt{expert\_evaluation\_dataset.jsonl}, the 10 novel CTE queries (zero-shot) in \texttt{cte\_evaluation\_dataset.jsonl} (together with the 5 main-evaluation queries, the CTE evaluation totals 15 queries), and the 20 variant-A queries are generated by \texttt{scripts/gen\_prototype\_evaluation.py} as \texttt{prototype\_evaluation\_dataset.jsonl}. The generalization evaluation datasets are \texttt{transfer\_evaluation\_dataset.jsonl} (B), \texttt{transfer\_obfuscated\_evaluation\_dataset.jsonl} (C), and \texttt{mp\_transfer\_evaluation\_dataset.jsonl} (D), used as paired inputs with the corresponding DBs of Step 2.

\paragraph{Step 4: LLM evaluation execution (external API, non-deterministic).}
As reproducibility conditions, the model, prompts, generation parameters, and execution conditions are fixed and recorded (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:sup_llm_params}). The API key (\texttt{OPENAI\_API\_KEY}) is an execution requirement.
(a)~Ablation: \texttt{scripts/eval\_ablation\_multirun.py --n-runs 5} runs 7 conditions $\times$ 100 queries five times, saving each run to \texttt{evaluation/ablation\_run\_\{1..5\}.json} and the mean $\pm$ SD to \texttt{ablation\_multirun\_stats.json} (single-run version: \texttt{eval\_ablation.py}, output \texttt{ablation\_results.json}).
(b)~Multi-axis metrics (Precision/F1/JOIN match/EM): \texttt{eval\_multiaxis\_metrics.py} $\to$ \texttt{multiaxis\_results.json}.
(c)~Independent set, CTE, and variant A: all run via \texttt{eval\_independent.py} with different \texttt{--dataset}/\texttt{--output} arguments (respectively \texttt{independent\_eval\_results.json}, \texttt{cte\_eval\_results.json}, \texttt{prototype\_eval\_results.json}).
(d)~Generalization B/C/D: \texttt{eval\_transfer.py}, \texttt{eval\_transfer\_obfuscated.py}, \texttt{eval\_mp\_transfer.py}.
(e)~Sensitivity analyses: \texttt{eval\_fewshot\_sensitivity.py} ($\to$ \texttt{fewshot\_sensitivity\_results.json}), \texttt{eval\_dict\_sensitivity.py} ($\to$ \texttt{dict\_sensitivity\_results.json}), \texttt{eval\_model\_comparison.py} ($\to$ \texttt{model\_comparison\_results.json}), \texttt{eval\_jp\_reranker\_vh.py} ($\to$ \texttt{jp\_reranker\_vh\_results.json}). The reranker A/B evaluation (\texttt{reranker\_eval\_results.json}) and the failure analysis (\texttt{failure\_analysis.json}) are bundled as stored evaluation outputs.
(f)~Safety: \texttt{scripts/verify\_safety\_cases.py} and the pytest test suite (\texttt{tests/}, 134 tests).

\paragraph{Step 5: Ablation statistical analysis.}
\texttt{scripts/compute\_ablation\_stats\_v2.py} computes each query's 5-run mean from \texttt{ablation\_run\_\{1..5\}.json} (paired data with $n=100$), and calculates the number of non-tied pairs $n_\mathrm{eff}$, two-sided exact sign-flip test p-values with the mean difference as the statistic (full enumeration for $n_\mathrm{eff}\leq20$, otherwise $2\times10^5$ Monte Carlo iterations with seed 20260602), paired rank-biserial correlations, and query-level bootstrap 95\% CIs (20{,}000 resamples), saving them to \texttt{evaluation/ablation\_significance\_v2.json}.

\paragraph{Step 6: Aggregation and figure generation (deterministic).}
\texttt{scripts/compute\_all\_figures.py} (result aggregation and SSOT generation) reads the evaluation JSONs of Steps 4--5 (five ablation runs, statistics, independent set, CTE, variant A, generalization B/C/D, reranker, multi-axis metrics, sensitivity analyses, model comparison, failure analysis) and generates \texttt{paper/paper\_data.json}, the single source of truth (SSOT) containing all published numbers and the figure source data (\texttt{figure\_source\_data}). The script first runs the pytest test suite (\texttt{tests/}, 134 tests) and aborts without generating \texttt{paper\_data.json} if even one test fails (test gate). Then \texttt{scripts/generate\_figures.py} reads only \texttt{paper\_data.json} and outputs the six figures (\texttt{ablation\_bar}, \texttt{fewshot\_sensitivity}, \texttt{dict\_sensitivity}, \texttt{multiaxis\_radar}, \texttt{model\_comparison}, \texttt{error\_distribution}) as PDF/PNG in \texttt{paper/figures/}. Thus both the published numbers and the figures are generated from the same finalized data that passed the test gate. These two steps can be reproduced in one shot by the distribution package's \texttt{reproduce.sh} (dependency installation $\to$ required-module check $\to$ DB startup $\to$ running both scripts).

\paragraph{Step 7: Numerical consistency verification and manuscript generation.}
\texttt{scripts/verify\_paper\_numbers.py --paper-dir paper} cross-checks the numbers in \texttt{paper\_data.json} against the published values in the manuscript TeX and fails on any mismatch. The manuscript is compiled with LuaLaTeX and BibTeX.

\paragraph{Evaluation and interpretation policy (governing Steps 3--7).}
The following is not a chronological step but the policy governing evaluation design and result interpretation. Table 1 (ablation) reports $\Delta$, $n_\mathrm{eff}$, exact p-values, effect sizes, and 95\% CIs together; for comparisons with small $n_\mathrm{eff}$ (no\_guard, no\_graph) significance is not assessed and interval estimates via CIs are used. Stratified $\Delta$ (especially VH, $n=20$) is treated as exploratory analysis. Because execution recall alone does not penalize over-retrieval, Precision, F1, and Exact Result-Set Match (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}) are interpreted together. Respecting the distinction between synthetic validation data (compound attributes) and OQMD-derived real data (pure-element reference energies) (Step 1), the evaluation target is the correctness of the extraction pipeline, not the materials engineering numbers themselves.
